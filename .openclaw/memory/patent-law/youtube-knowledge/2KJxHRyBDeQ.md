# What are common mistakes innovators make in their provisional patent filings?

**Channel**: Stanford Biodesign
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2KJxHRyBDeQ
**Published**: 2014-10-23

---

## Overview

This short interview-style video from Stanford Biodesign features an expert explaining the most common mistakes innovators make when drafting provisional patent applications. The central theme is under-inclusion: inventors tend to describe their specific implementation rather than thinking broadly about what they would want to protect, including all variations and alternatives a competitor might use to replicate the value of the invention without copying it exactly.

The expert uses the "pen with blue ink" example to illustrate: an inventor who only discloses a pen with blue ink loses the ability to claim a pen with any kind of ink, or a writing implement of any modality. The document must be treated as a "word bank" — every term, concept, and variation that might be needed in future claims must appear in the provisional. Because new content cannot be added after filing, under-inclusive provisionals permanently limit the scope of subsequent non-provisional claims.

A practical solution offered is filing multiple provisionals over time — even weekly or monthly — to incrementally add new features, alternative embodiments, and broader framings as they develop. The goal is not merely to protect the current invention, but to build a legal barrier around the entire competitive space the invention occupies, including what competitors might build to achieve the same outcome.

## Key Takeaways

- The most common provisional patent mistake is under-inclusion — not thinking broadly enough about what to protect
- A provisional is a "word bank" — every term and concept needed in future claims must appear in the provisional
- New content cannot be added after filing; under-inclusive provisionals permanently limit non-provisional claim scope
- Think beyond the specific implementation to describe variations, alternatives, and broader categories
- Draft the patent to protect a competitive space, not just the specific invention as currently built
- Multiple provisionals can be filed over time (even monthly) to incrementally broaden the disclosure
- A technical enablement standard applies: describe the invention in enough detail for someone to make and use it
- The question to ask is: what alternatives would I be offended to see in my competitive marketplace?

## Best For

Innovators and early-stage inventors who want concise, expert guidance on the single biggest mistake in provisional patent applications — under-inclusion — and how to think broadly enough to build effective patent protection.
