## Overview

This video features a live Q&A discussion between patent attorney J.D. Houvener and trademark attorney Matt, covering key differences between patents and trademarks with a focus on practical questions from inventors and entrepreneurs. The conversation covers assignment and transfer rules, the difference between utility and design patents, how to find and work with a patent broker, and the Foreign Equivalence Doctrine in trademark law.

A critical distinction covered is the transfer of rights: trademark applications cannot be assigned to a third party before the trademark owner has actually gone to market with the product or service, because a trademark must be tied to actual commercial use. Patent applications, by contrast, can be bought and sold even while pending — though pending applications carry the risk of never being granted. Another important topic is when to choose a design patent versus a utility patent. The video advises pursuing a utility patent first because once the utility application has been filed (but before it is granted), the inventor can still file for design patent protection on the aesthetic elements — maximizing both protection windows.

On the topic of patent brokers, the attorneys advise checking the broker's track record, understanding the process they use to reach buyers and licensees, and carefully reading the "tail clause" in the broker agreement — a provision stating that deals consummated within a period after the contract expires still generate a commission for the broker.

## Key Takeaways

- Trademarks cannot be assigned to third parties until the owner has actually used the mark in commerce.
- Patent applications (even pending ones) can be bought and sold at any stage.
- Utility patents protect function and how something works; design patents protect only appearance.
- File for a utility patent first — you can still add design patent protection before the utility patent is granted.
- The Foreign Equivalence Doctrine can bar trademark registration if the foreign word translates to a merely descriptive English term.
- When evaluating a patent broker, ask for success stories, track record, communication process, and review the contract carefully.
- Check the broker's "tail clause" — commission may still be owed for deals that close after the broker agreement expires.
- A patent broker who is also a patent attorney has significant advantages in valuing and negotiating IP assets.

## Best For

This video is most useful for inventors and entrepreneurs who want to understand the key legal differences between patents and trademarks, and are considering assigning, licensing, or brokering their intellectual property.
