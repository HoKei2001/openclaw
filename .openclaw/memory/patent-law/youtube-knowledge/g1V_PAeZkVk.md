## Overview

Patent attorney J.D. Houvener of Bold Patents Law Firm explains the patent prosecution process from application filing through final rejection, using a real-world patent example (a machine tool invention by Okuma Corporation) to illustrate how applicants can and often do overcome final rejections. The video is part of a live Q&A show called "Bold Today" and also addresses a viewer question on the constitutional basis for patent protection.

## Key Takeaways

- A "final rejection" from the USPTO is not truly the end of the road — inventors have multiple options to continue prosecution, and most patents that ultimately issue go through at least one rejection before allowance
- After a final rejection, applicants can file a Request for Continued Examination (RCE), paying a fee (typically around $500, or less for small entities) to get a fresh start with the examiner including a new non-final rejection and the ability to submit new claim amendments
- Conducting examiner interviews at key stages — both before and after final rejections — is a highly effective strategy for understanding what changes are needed and narrowing the path to allowance
- In the real-world Okuma machine tool example, the applicant went through a non-final rejection, made claim amendments, received a final rejection, filed an RCE, went through another round, and ultimately got a notice of allowance by more precisely defining the tool spindle device structure
- On the traditional patent prosecution timeline, a first office action can take 6–12 months (or over a year) for standard track filings, but Track One prioritized examination typically shortens this to 3–6 months
- The constitutional basis for the US patent system is Article 1, Section 8 of the US Constitution, which incentivizes inventors to publicly disclose their inventions in exchange for a time-limited monopoly
- After receiving a notice of allowance, inventors should consider filing continuation or child patent applications to broaden their portfolio
