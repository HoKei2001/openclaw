## Overview

This Michigan Engineering lecture (part 12 of a 62-part IP series) provides a detailed academic treatment of the non-obviousness standard for U.S. patent law. The video distinguishes non-obviousness from novelty: while novelty requires only one prior art reference to contain all elements of a claim, non-obviousness looks at the prior art as a whole and asks whether the combination of differences between the claimed invention and prior art would be apparent to a person of ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was made.

The video covers the key legal precedents and examiner guidelines for finding an invention obvious, including combining known elements using known methods to achieve a predictable result; substituting one element for another to achieve a predictable result; and "obvious to try" (where there is a finite number of identifiable solutions and it would be predictable that any of them would work). The 2007 KSR v. Teleflex Supreme Court decision is noted for eliminating the former requirement that prior art explicitly suggest combining references — the standard is now one of common sense and predictability.

To rebut obviousness, the video explains secondary considerations (also called objective indicia of non-obviousness): commercial success of the invention, long-felt but unsolved need in the industry, failure of others to solve the problem, and unexpected results. The presenter emphasizes that the best evidence includes concrete data — commercial sales figures, expert testimony, industry publications — rather than just attorney arguments. These secondary considerations are especially useful when the technical arguments about non-obviousness are weak or uncertain, because they demonstrate real-world proof that the invention was not predictable.

## Key Takeaways

- Non-obviousness is judged from the perspective of a person of ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was made
- Unlike novelty, obviousness looks at the prior art as a whole — multiple references can be combined
- Three classic bases for obviousness: combining known elements by known methods, substituting one known element for another, and "obvious to try"
- KSR v. Teleflex (2007): eliminated the requirement for an explicit teaching/suggestion/motivation to combine prior art references
- Secondary considerations to rebut obviousness: commercial success, long-felt need, failure of others, unexpected results
- Concrete evidence (sales data, publications, expert testimony) is far more persuasive than attorney argument alone
- After KSR, the obviousness standard is based on common sense and predictability rather than formal logical requirements
