# Patents: How to Respond to an Office Action - Part I

**Published**: 2012-11-13

## Overview

Patent attorney Michael Feigin delivers a practitioner-focused presentation on responding to patent office actions, notable for its emphasis on how the response process actually begins at the application drafting stage — not when the office action arrives. The video opens with a real-world "crazy office action" example where an examiner cited a figure that bore no visible relationship to the claimed element, illustrating that examiners operate under significant time pressure and sometimes issue inadequate or unclear rejections. This context-setting observation shapes the entire approach to responding: be concise, be precise, and never assume the examiner's position is well-reasoned.

The application drafting section covers practical strategies for preempting rejections: define every key claim term explicitly (particularly terms like "substantially" that have been the subject of Section 112 rejections), include alternative embodiments described as alternatives rather than "preferred" embodiments (to preserve infringement scope for non-preferred alternatives), and prepare backup claim language and variations in the specification that can be used to amend claims without adding new matter. These preparation steps are framed as investments that pay off when drafting responses later. The background section of the application is highlighted as an opportunity to set the examiner's expectations and frame the prior art favorably, while being cautious not to admit what the invention is or make the solution obvious.

For the response itself, Feigin emphasizes making arguments short and concise, maintaining a positive professional relationship with the examiner (who is under significant pressure), and using examiner interviews extensively to reach agreement before committing to written positions. Including relevant drawing figures directly in the response document is recommended as a powerful tool for making visual comparisons clear, particularly when arguing that a cited reference does not actually disclose the claimed element.

## Key Takeaways

- Effective office action responses begin at the application drafting stage — define key terms, include alternative embodiments, and prepare backup claim language in advance
- Avoid using "preferred embodiment" language; courts have interpreted it to narrow infringement scope for non-preferred alternatives — use "alternative embodiment" instead
- Define claim terms explicitly using language like "defined as" to resist the examiner's Broadest Reasonable Interpretation (BRI) reading
- Examiners operate under significant time pressure and sometimes issue inadequate or unclear rejections; always read the cited art carefully before assuming the rejection is correct
- Arguments in responses should be short, concise, and direct — examiner interviews are more effective for resolving complex disagreements than lengthy written arguments
- Copying relevant drawing figures directly into the response (your figure vs. the prior art figure) is a powerful visual argument tool
- Background sections can frame the prior art and set favorable context, but must be drafted carefully to avoid admitting what the invention is or making the solution appear obvious

## Best For

Patent practitioners and inventors who want to understand how to proactively design patent applications for survivability under examination and how to craft clear, effective responses when office actions arrive.
