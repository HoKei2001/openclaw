## Overview

Inventor and licensing expert Stephen Key shares practical, experience-based advice on how to find the right patent attorney or agent and avoid common pitfalls that leave inventors with worthless patents. Speaking from years of personal experience obtaining and monetizing patents — including defending his portfolio against Lego in federal court — Stephen provides a checklist of red flags and best practices for working with patent professionals. He emphasizes this is practical business advice, not legal counsel.

The video covers multiple warning signs to watch for when evaluating a patent practitioner: using fear tactics to rush filings, failing to include office action response costs in their quotes, refusing to conduct interviews with patent examiners, performing their own patent search rather than using an independent third party, and writing patents that are difficult for the inventor to understand. Stephen argues that if you cannot read and understand your own patent application, something is wrong.

A central theme is that inventors should arrive well-prepared when working with a patent attorney. Providing a working prototype, marketing materials that highlight competitive differentiation, and knowledge of similar products on the market will help the attorney focus on what actually needs protection. Stephen also reiterates his consistent advice: start with a provisional patent application before committing to the expensive non-provisional.

## Key Takeaways

- Avoid patent attorneys who use fear to pressure inventors into filing quickly.
- Always get a full cost estimate upfront that explicitly includes office action response fees.
- Choose practitioners who actively conduct examiner interviews — it is a key strategy for getting patents allowed.
- Patent searches should be done by an independent third party, not the same attorney filing your application.
- Ask to read a patent your attorney has written before hiring them — it should be clear and easy to understand.
- Have the attorney explain how specific claims in the application protect the features that matter to you.
- Start with a provisional patent application ($1,500–$2,500 with an attorney) before committing to a full non-provisional ($25,000+).
- Your attorney is only as good as the information you provide — come prepared with a prototype, market research, and competitive analysis.

## Best For

Inventors who are ready to work with a patent professional and want a checklist to identify trustworthy, effective practitioners versus those who may not have their best interests at heart.
