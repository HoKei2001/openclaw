## Overview

This brief guide from The Slop Temple offers a straightforward overview of how to file a non-provisional patent application. The video emphasizes that the non-provisional application is the crucial step for securing an official filing date and initiating the formal examination process. Unlike the provisional patent application, the non-provisional requires comprehensive, legally precise documentation and sets the patent journey in motion.

Three key components must be included in the application: a complete specification containing a thorough written description of the invention and at least one claim defining the scope of protection sought; clear and detailed drawings that visually represent every feature of the invention; and an oath or declaration from each inventor affirming their role in creating the invention. Filing is typically done electronically through the USPTO's secure online system, and fees vary depending on entity size — micro, small, or large entity — with significant discounts for smaller filers.

After filing, the application enters examination by a patent examiner who assesses it for novelty, non-obviousness, and utility. Office actions — communications from the examiner identifying issues or requiring amendments — are a normal and expected part of the process. Successfully navigating office action responses is critical to getting the patent granted.

## Key Takeaways

- The non-provisional application establishes the official filing date and begins formal examination
- Required components: complete specification with at least one claim, clear drawings, and inventor oath/declaration
- File electronically through the USPTO's online system; fees vary by entity size (micro, small, or large)
- Inventors can file themselves or work with a registered patent attorney for professional guidance
- After filing, anticipate office actions from the examiner requiring responses or claim amendments
- Novelty, non-obviousness, and utility are the three substantive patentability standards reviewed during examination
- Responding to office actions is common and necessary — it is a normal part of the patent process
