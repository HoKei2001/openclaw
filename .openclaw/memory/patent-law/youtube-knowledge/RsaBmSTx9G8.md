# What Makes an Invention too Obvious to Patent?

## Overview

Silicon Valley patent attorney John Ferrell explains the legal concept of non-obviousness — one of the three core requirements for patentability — in clear, accessible terms. Using everyday examples like bicycle handlebar streamers and chocolate chip cookies, he illustrates when combining known elements produces an obvious, predictable result versus when it produces a surprising, non-obvious one.

The video explains the legal standard used to evaluate obviousness: the hypothetical "person of ordinary skill in the art" (POSITA). Patent law evaluates whether the claimed invention would have been obvious to this fictitious person — someone with typical training and experience in the relevant field at the time the invention was made. This standard is intentionally flexible, and in real disputes, a federal court judge assigns specific skill levels and traits to this person and uses them to evaluate the claims.

Ferrell walks through a Supreme Court-level example to demonstrate how this plays out in litigation: if an inventor holds a patent and someone infringes it, the infringer can argue the patent is invalid for obviousness. The judge would then define the POSITA and determine whether that person would have found the combination obvious. Two key Supreme Court cases are recommended for deeper study: Graham v. John Deere (the foundational case establishing the obviousness analysis factors) and KSR v. Teleflex (which refined the analysis around combining known elements).

## Key Takeaways

- Non-obviousness is one of the three requirements for patentability, alongside novelty and utility.
- An invention is non-obvious if combining known elements produces unexpected or unpredictable results.
- Adding known decorative elements to an existing product (like streamers to a bicycle) is typically considered obvious and not patentable.
- The legal standard uses a fictitious "person of ordinary skill in the art" (POSITA) to evaluate obviousness objectively.
- Courts assign specific training and experience levels to the POSITA based on the field of the invention.
- Obviousness is evaluated at the time the invention was made, not at the time of the lawsuit.
- Graham v. John Deere and KSR v. Teleflex are the key Supreme Court cases governing non-obviousness analysis.
- A strong explanation of why the invention is non-obvious can be persuasive with a patent examiner during prosecution.

## Best For

Inventors and entrepreneurs who want to understand whether their invention clears the non-obviousness bar before investing in a patent application.
