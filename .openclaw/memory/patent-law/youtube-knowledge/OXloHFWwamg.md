## Overview

This video presents a critical, contrarian perspective on software patents for startups, arguing that for most software companies — especially early-stage startups — patents are more of a distraction than a strategic asset. The presenter shares a first-hand experience from founding a presentation software startup (SL) in 2014, where the team initially filed a provisional patent application before a meeting with a legal team that explained the invention was essentially too abstract to patent. The content-design separation concept couldn't be patented, the algorithm was too easily circumvented, and a UI patent would only be enforceable against an exact visual copy.

The video uses real-world examples to illustrate the limits of software patents: TikTok's infinite scrolling, Netflix's recommendation algorithm, and Snapchat Stories were never patented or couldn't be. In contrast, Amazon's one-click buying patent (granted 1997) is cited as one of very few successful software patents — it was concrete, novel, and resulted in Apple licensing it and Barnes & Noble being sued. Google's PageRank algorithm was patented but never enforced, and the disclosure may have actually inspired competitors. The presenter catalogs what is generally not patentable in software: abstract ideas, basic algorithms, business methods, basic UIs, standard data organization methods, and standard mobile app functionality. What might be patentable includes novel compression or encryption algorithms, unique gesture recognition, and non-obvious AR implementations.

The video concludes that for most software startups, the high cost of obtaining a patent ($10,000+), the years of wait time, and the potential $1–4 million in litigation costs to enforce it make patents a poor strategic investment — unless the startup's entire valuation model is built on a patent portfolio for acquisition purposes.

## Key Takeaways

- A provisional patent ("patent pending") costs ~$200 to file but has no protection value until converted to a full patent ($10,000+)
- Most software concepts are too abstract to patent: abstract ideas, basic algorithms, business methods, and standard UI patterns cannot be patented
- Amazon's one-click patent is one of very few software patents that actually worked to stop competitors and generate licensing revenue
- Enforcing a software patent requires proving damages in court — a process that starts at $1 million and typically costs $4 million
- Google's PageRank patent was granted but never enforced, and its public disclosure may have inspired competing algorithms
- Patentable software elements include novel compression/encryption algorithms, unique gesture recognition, and non-obvious AR methods
- For most software startups, patents can be a costly distraction; focus resources on execution unless your business model depends on a patent portfolio

## Best For

Software startup founders and developers who want a realistic, experience-based assessment of when software patents add value versus when they are a waste of time and money.
