## Overview

This video from goldsteinpatent explains the two patent requirements — novelty and non-obviousness — with an emphasis on practical understanding of what "non-obvious" means and how it applies. The presenter distinguishes novelty (nothing exactly like the invention in the prior art) from non-obviousness (the differences from prior art cannot be an obvious leap to someone skilled in the field). An invention can be novel but still be rejected as obvious if what makes it different would be something practitioners in the field would naturally think to do.

To illustrate non-obviousness, the presenter uses the example of a children's clothes hanger: making a standard hanger smaller for children's clothing may be novel (no existing hanger is exactly that size), but it would be obvious to anyone who manufactures hangers — they know they can make them any size, and choosing a particular size is just a straightforward adjustment. This kind of obvious variation does not deserve patent protection. The threshold for non-obviousness creates genuine difficulty for the untrained eye, and distinguishing between what is and is not obvious often requires expert guidance from a patent attorney who can assess the closest prior art and evaluate whether the differences are truly surprising or expected.

The video concludes by setting up the next topic in the series: how to determine the closest prior art to your invention, which is the necessary first step to assessing non-obviousness. This video is part of a patent education series aimed at helping inventors understand what makes an invention patentable before investing in the application process.

## Key Takeaways

- Novelty: there is nothing exactly like your invention in the prior art
- Non-obviousness: the differences between your invention and the prior art cannot be an obvious step to someone skilled in the field
- An invention can be novel but still fail because it would be obvious to make the change that distinguishes it from prior art
- The clothes hanger example: changing size is a simple, known operation — obvious to manufacturers, therefore not patentable
- Non-obviousness requires "unexpectedness" — the difference must surprise someone familiar with the technology
- Determining whether something is obvious often requires the opinion of a skilled patent attorney
- The next step in assessing non-obviousness is identifying the closest prior art to your invention

## Best For

Inventors new to the patent system who want an intuitive, example-based explanation of the difference between novelty and non-obviousness before deciding whether to pursue a patent application.
