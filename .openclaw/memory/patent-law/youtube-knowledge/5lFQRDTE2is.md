# Top 5 Patent Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

**Channel**: Innovent Law
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5lFQRDTE2is
**Published**: 2020-08-31

---

## Overview

Patent attorney Karima Gulick of Innovent Law presents the five most common patent mistakes she sees inventors and business owners make, with practical guidance for avoiding each one. The video is organized around real scenarios drawn from her practice, making each mistake concrete and recognizable. The overarching message is that patent protection is a business decision, and the timing and quality of that decision can make or break a product's commercial future.

The five mistakes covered are: (1) selling the invention for more than 12 months before obtaining patent protection — triggering the statutory on-sale bar that permanently bars U.S. patent rights; (2) disclosing the invention publicly for more than 12 months before filing — the public disclosure bar works identically; (3) not conducting a proper prior art search, which leads to ordering inventory or making business investments in products that turn out to already exist; (4) filing a bad provisional application — a thin, one-page document with no drawings that provides virtually no patent protection despite creating a false sense of security; and (5) spending too much or too little on patent protection — the first leading to premature investment before market validation (the presenter cites a Shark Tank example of $750,000 spent before a first sale), and the second resulting in a provisional that is too thin to be enabling.

## Key Takeaways

- Selling an invention for more than 12 months before filing triggers the statutory on-sale bar, permanently blocking U.S. patent rights
- Publicly disclosing an invention for more than 12 months before filing similarly bars patent protection
- Not conducting a prior art search can lead to ordering large inventory quantities of a product that already exists and cannot be patented
- A thin, one-page provisional application with no drawings provides virtually no patent protection despite appearing to establish priority
- Spending $750,000+ on patent protection before making a first sale is unwise — patenting is a business decision tied to market validation
- Spending too little on a provisional (e.g., $150) typically results in an enabling disclosure that is insufficient to support later non-provisional claims
- Always consult a patent professional before making sales, disclosures, or large inventory commitments
- Quality and timing of patent protection decisions matter more than having a patent application on file at any cost

## Best For

Inventors and product-based business owners who want to understand the most costly and common patent timing and quality mistakes before they make any public disclosures or business investments.
