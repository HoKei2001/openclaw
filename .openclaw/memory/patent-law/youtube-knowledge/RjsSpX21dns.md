# Is Adding Extra Features Enough to Avoid Patent Infringement?

**Channel**: Avoiding Common Legal Mistakes
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RjsSpX21dns
**Published**: 2025-07-19

---

## Overview

This video addresses a common misconception among inventors and product developers: that adding extra features to an existing patented invention is sufficient to avoid patent infringement. The presenter explains that this is generally not true under U.S. patent law, which grants patent holders exclusive rights to make, use, sell, or import an invention as defined in its claims. The central legal principle at work is the "all elements rule" — if a new product includes every element (or an equivalent of every element) recited in any claim of a patent, infringement is established regardless of whatever additional features the product may have.

The video explains that avoiding infringement requires more than addition — it requires removing or altering the patented elements themselves so that the new product no longer satisfies all elements of any relevant patent claim. Simply adding new functionality on top of a patented foundation does not escape infringement. The patent grants a negative right to exclude others from the claimed invention in its entirety, not just from the exact version described in the drawings.

Practical guidance is offered: conduct a thorough patent search, carefully analyze the specific claims of any relevant patents (not just the title or abstract), and consider seeking legal advice for design-around strategies. Filing a provisional patent application for a genuinely new invention can help establish priority, but provisional status does not provide any enforceable rights.

## Key Takeaways

- Adding extra features to a patented invention generally does not avoid patent infringement
- The "all elements rule" means infringement is established if a product includes all elements of any one claim, regardless of added features
- Patents grant a negative right to exclude others from the claimed invention — not just the exact version shown in drawings
- Avoiding infringement requires removing or altering patented claim elements, not just adding new ones
- Even a product that looks very different from the patented device can infringe if it embodies all claim elements
- Courts focus on whether the accused product includes all elements of any claim of the patent
- Design-around strategies require careful analysis of the specific patent claims, not just the general concept
- Seeking legal advice is essential when attempting to design around an existing patent
