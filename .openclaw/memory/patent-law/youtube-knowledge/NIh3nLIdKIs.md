# Provisional Patent Application or Non-Provisional?

**Channel**: Patents Demystified
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NIh3nLIdKIs
**Published**: 2021-03-18

---

## Overview

Patent attorney Dylan Adams (author of "Patents Demystified," used at Harvard, Stanford, and MIT) provides a thorough comparison of the two ways to start the patent process: filing a non-provisional patent application directly, or starting with a provisional patent application. The non-provisional is the formal application that enters the examination queue at the USPTO, waiting 1–3 years before a patent examiner begins review. About 95% of applications receive rejections, and the process involves back-and-forth negotiation with the examiner before a patent can issue.

The provisional patent application, by contrast, is simply a placeholder — it establishes a priority date, lasts exactly one year (non-extendable), is never formally examined, and must be converted to a non-provisional within that year to maintain the priority date. The USPTO accepts virtually any document as a provisional, but quality matters: a poor provisional may not support the claims later added in the non-provisional, potentially undermining the priority date. The provisional does not require the rigid formatting of a non-provisional, making it more affordable and faster to prepare.

Adams strongly recommends provisionals for startups because they provide the same "patent pending" status for a year while allowing the invention to evolve. Startups can file updates to the provisional as the invention changes — something impossible once a non-provisional is filed. The year of patent-pending status allows time to test the market, attract investors, and refine the product. If the non-provisional is filed first and the invention changes, new features require a completely new (and expensive) application, which is often impractical.

## Key Takeaways

- Two starting paths: file a non-provisional directly (enters examination queue) or start with a provisional (12-month placeholder, not examined)
- Provisionals expire after exactly one year — the term cannot be extended
- The USPTO accepts any document as a provisional, but quality determines what priority date you actually get credit for
- Provisionals are strongly recommended for startups: they're cheaper, less formal, and allow the invention to evolve during the year
- Both a non-provisional and a provisional provide "patent pending" status
- Once a non-provisional is filed, no new features can be added — all changes require a new application
- Starting with a provisional gives flexibility to update the application before committing to the full non-provisional

## Best For

Startups, first-time inventors, and entrepreneurs deciding whether to start the patent process with a provisional or non-provisional application, particularly when the invention may still be evolving.
