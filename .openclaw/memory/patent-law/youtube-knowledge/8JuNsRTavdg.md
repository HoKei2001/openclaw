# How Can A Provisional Patent Application Waste Money?

## Overview

This video explains the specific ways in which a provisional patent application can become a waste of money rather than a strategic investment. While a provisional is intended as a low-cost method to secure an early filing date, several common pitfalls can negate its value entirely — and in some cases double the total cost of obtaining patent protection.

The primary ways a provisional wastes money are: filing with an incomplete or vague description that forces the inventor to essentially restart during non-provisional preparation (doubling legal costs); having an insufficient disclosure that fails to support the priority date for the non-provisional, weakening patent rights and leaving the invention vulnerable to competitors; failing to follow up with a non-provisional application before the 12-month deadline expires, losing both the filing fee and the priority date; and misunderstanding the provisional as granting enforceable rights, when in fact it grants none. The USPTO does not examine provisional applications, so there is no guarantee of patentability, meaning money can be spent even when the invention ultimately has no path to a granted patent.

The video frames the provisional as a tool in a larger strategy — when used correctly with a thorough description and a clear plan for the non-provisional conversion, it saves money and time. When used carelessly as a cheap placeholder, it creates the illusion of protection while delivering none.

## Key Takeaways

- Rushing into a provisional with an incomplete description often requires starting over for the non-provisional, doubling legal costs
- An insufficient disclosure may not support the non-provisional's priority date, weakening patent rights against competitors
- The provisional expires after 12 months; missing the non-provisional deadline loses both the fee and the priority date
- The USPTO does not examine provisional applications — there is no guarantee the invention is patentable
- A provisional does not grant enforceable rights; it cannot stop others from copying the invention
- Money spent on a provisional for a non-patentable invention is entirely lost
- The provisional is useful only as part of a larger patent strategy with a clear conversion plan
- Working with a patent professional ensures the provisional is thorough enough to support the non-provisional application
