## Overview

This video provides a clear, structured introduction to performing a prior art search before filing a patent application. It begins by explaining the purpose: to understand the landscape of existing ideas, confirm that an invention is genuinely new and non-obvious, and build a stronger patent application. The presenter walks through the key steps in methodical order, from defining the invention and selecting keywords, to searching patent databases and non-patent literature, to carefully reviewing and comparing results.

The recommended databases are the major free resources — the USPTO, the European Patent Office, and WIPO — which together contain millions of patents and patent applications. The video emphasizes that non-patent literature (scientific articles, technical journals, product manuals, and online forums) must also be searched, since publicly available information that is not patented can still constitute prior art that undermines novelty. Detailed record-keeping throughout the search process is advised, as documentation of sources reviewed can be valuable if the invention's novelty is questioned later.

The presenter acknowledges that thorough searches can take days to weeks depending on the invention's complexity, and recommends working with a patent attorney or agent to interpret results and identify issues that may affect patentability. A well-executed prior art search is framed not merely as a box-checking exercise but as a strategic investment that saves money, shapes stronger claims, and produces a clearer picture of what the invention truly contributes.

## Key Takeaways

- Clearly define the invention's main features, functions, and the problem it solves before beginning a search
- Write down keywords that describe the invention as the starting point for database queries
- Identify relevant patent classifications (CPC/IPC codes) to narrow searches to the correct technology area
- Search USPTO, EPO, and WIPO databases — all free and containing millions of patents and applications
- Non-patent literature (scientific articles, journals, product manuals, forums) must also be searched
- Compare found documents carefully to the invention to assess if new features or improvements distinguish it
- Keep detailed records of all sources and documents reviewed during the search
- Working with a patent attorney or agent helps interpret results and identify patentability issues

Inventors who want a clear, step-by-step framework for conducting a prior art search across multiple databases before filing their patent application.
