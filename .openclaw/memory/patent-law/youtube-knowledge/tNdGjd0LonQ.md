## Overview

Patent attorney Adam Diament of Diamond Patent Law dedicates this podcast episode to one of the most common rejection types encountered in patent prosecution: the Section 102 anticipation rejection, also known as a lack-of-novelty or anticipation rejection. The episode explains the legal standard for this rejection type in plain terms, then provides a structured framework for responding depending on whether the prior art actually discloses the claimed elements or not.

The Section 102 standard is clarified upfront: "anticipation" in patent law means that each and every limitation of a claim is found in a single prior art reference. This is a black-and-white standard — if even one limitation is not present in the cited reference, the rejection fails and can be argued away. Diament walks through a concrete scenario using a mechanical pencil: if the examiner claims a cited patent discloses a button on the side but the cited patent actually shows the button at the tip, the prior art does not anticipate the claim as written, and the response should argue this specific factual difference limitation by limitation. The response structure is detailed: cite the MPEP section, cite relevant case law (Richardson v. Suzuki, Verdeo Gaul Brothers, Rockwell Corp), and then clearly state why the cited art fails to disclose each required element.

The second scenario covers what to do when the prior art does disclose all claimed elements: amend the claims to add a new limitation not present in the cited reference, such as an additional component or feature described elsewhere in the original application. Diament emphasizes that claims can only be amended using matter already disclosed in the original application (no new matter), and cautions against ever admitting in the response that the cited art actually discloses what the examiner said — due to prosecution history estoppel, such admissions can be used against the patent holder later.

## Key Takeaways

- A Section 102 anticipation rejection means the examiner claims each and every element of the claim is found in a single prior art reference
- The correct first step is to read the cited reference and verify whether the examiner's element-by-element mapping is accurate — examiners sometimes misread or overstate what the reference discloses
- If the prior art genuinely does not disclose all elements, argue the specific limitation that is missing, citing MPEP section 2131 and relevant case law
- If the prior art does disclose all claimed elements, amend the claims to add a new limitation from the original application that the prior art does not teach
- Keep responses clear and concise — shorter, well-targeted arguments are more effective than lengthy briefs for anticipation rejections
- Never concede in the response that the prior art discloses what the examiner said, even when amending claims — prosecution history estoppel can turn such admissions against the patent holder
- Overcoming a 102 rejection may lead to a 103 obviousness rejection; anticipation responses are generally easier to write than obviousness responses because the standard is more clear-cut

## Best For

Inventors and patent practitioners who have received a Section 102 anticipation rejection and need a practical, structured guide for analyzing the rejection and drafting an effective response.
