# How to Patent A Design Idea: Design Patents Explained

**Channel**: Patents Demystified
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_G2UhjP3IPM
**Published**: 2021-03-23

---

## Overview

Dylan Adams of Patents Demystified delivers a thorough and clarifying explanation of how designs can be protected by patents, opening with the critical point that inventors often confuse "design patent" with "patent for a design" — and that this confusion leads them to choose the wrong protection type. Adams explains that when most inventors say they want to patent a design, they often actually need a utility patent, not a design patent, because the functionality and variations of a design are what's commercially important to protect.

The video maps all four IP protection types to design protection: trade secrets (pre-disclosure), copyrights (artistic works), trademarks (logos and trade dress), and patents. Within patents, Adams distinguishes clearly between design patents and utility patents. Design patents protect a single specific ornamental appearance — they consist entirely of line drawings from multiple perspectives and are limited to that exact design. They are relatively easy to get approved and less expensive to obtain, but their narrow scope means a competitor can easily design around them by making minor changes. Utility patents, by contrast, use words to define functional claims and can cover a wide range of design variations, methods of use, and methods of manufacture — making them far more powerful but also harder and more expensive to obtain.

Adams explains when each type is appropriate: design patents work best when the specific visual appearance of a product is its key differentiating factor (furniture, consumer goods with distinctive forms); utility patents work best when functionality and variation matter; and sometimes filing both is the right strategy — guaranteeing some protection via the design patent while pursuing broader utility patent protection simultaneously. He closes by recommending consultation with a patent attorney given the product-by-product nature of the decision.

## Key Takeaways

- "Design patent" and "patent for a design" are often confused — most inventors who want to protect a design actually need a utility patent
- Design patents cover exactly one specific ornamental appearance through line drawings from multiple views; they are easy to obtain but narrowly protective
- Utility patents cover functionality and design variations through words and claims; they are more powerful but more expensive, time-consuming, and harder to obtain
- Design patents are a good fit when the exact visual appearance is the key differentiating feature of the product (e.g., furniture, distinctive consumer product shapes)
- Utility patents protect against design-arounds by covering variations and functional mechanisms, not just a single look
- Filing both a design patent and a utility patent on the same product is sometimes the optimal strategy — certainty from the design patent while pursuing broader utility protection
- The right type of protection is highly product-specific and should be evaluated with a patent attorney before filing

## Best For

Inventors and product designers who want a clear, accurate explanation of when to use a design patent versus a utility patent and why the choice is not as obvious as the names suggest.
