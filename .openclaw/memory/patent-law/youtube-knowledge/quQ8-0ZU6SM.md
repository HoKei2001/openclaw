## Overview

Triangle IP answers a common question from inventors and clients: what happens when the USPTO issues an office action rejecting a patent application, and how can applicants overcome different types of rejections? The video provides a practical survey of the main categories of patent rejections, offers strategic advice on handling them, and includes a useful tip about analyzing examiner allowance rates using tools like Triangle IP's examiner analytics.

## Key Takeaways

- About 85% of patent applications in the US receive at least one office action rejecting some or all claims; roughly two-thirds of all applications ultimately issue as patents, so rejection should not trigger panic
- The main categories of patent rejections are: 112 rejections (written description or claim clarity issues), 101 subject matter eligibility rejections (including the Alice case for abstract ideas and business methods), 102 novelty rejections (a single prior art reference teaches everything in the claim), and 103 obviousness rejections (multiple references combined teach all claim elements)
- Novelty (102) rejections are generally easier to overcome because there will typically be some meaningful difference between the claimed invention and the cited prior art that can be highlighted or added to the claims
- Obviousness (103) rejections are the most common and difficult; they require demonstrating that the combination of prior art references is not straightforward — the "1+1=2" approach must be challenged with evidence of synergy or unexpected results (1+1=3)
- When crafting claims, avoid overly broad language that sweeps in common technology from unrelated fields; narrowing claims to the specific industry and application area reduces the universe of prior art the examiner is likely to find
- Look up examiner analytics before or during prosecution: an examiner with a 90% allowance rate requires a different strategy than one who has not allowed a patent in two years; tools like Triangle IP's examiner analytics can be invaluable
- Responding to office actions requires specialized legal knowledge; lay inventors cannot effectively analyze and respond to most rejections without help from a patent attorney, agent, or registered counsel
