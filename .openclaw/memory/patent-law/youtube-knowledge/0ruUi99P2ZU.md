## Overview

Registered patent attorney Derek Fahey explains one of his favorite arguments for overcoming obviousness rejections (35 USC 103) — the "frustration of purpose" argument. The video provides a concise, practical explanation of how obviousness rejections work and how this specific legal argument can be used to defeat a rejection when the combination of cited prior art references would render the primary reference inoperable.

## Key Takeaways

- Obviousness rejections under 35 USC 103 are extremely common and arise when an examiner cites two or more prior art references whose combined elements teach each element of the claimed invention
- The first step when receiving an obviousness rejection is to carefully read each cited reference and verify whether the examiner has correctly identified what each reference actually teaches; if not, this failure to establish a prima facie case of obviousness is grounds for withdrawal of the rejection
- The "frustration of purpose" argument holds that if combining the secondary reference into the primary reference would actually render the primary reference inoperable or destroy its function, there is no valid motivation to combine — and therefore the prima facie case of obviousness fails
- Example: if an invention has a fan blowing down from the top and a fan blowing in from the side, and the primary reference shows a module where adding a side fan would cause damage or make the device non-functional, arguing that the combination would "frustrate the purpose" of the primary reference is a strong basis for rejecting the obviousness rejection
- This argument is particularly effective in mechanical and software arts and is used frequently by the presenter
- Even if prior art references do teach each element of the claimed invention, the applicant must find a basis to argue that the combination should not have been made — the frustration of purpose argument is one such basis
