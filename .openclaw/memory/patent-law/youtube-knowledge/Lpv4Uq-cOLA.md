## Overview

This UpCounsel video provides a concise overview of patent licensing — what it is, why inventors use it, the two main types, and how to navigate the licensing process. Patent licensing is presented as a powerful tool for inventors and businesses who want to monetize their intellectual property without taking on the burden of manufacturing or marketing a product themselves.

A patent license is defined as a legal agreement allowing someone other than the patent owner to make, use, or sell an invention protected by that patent, while the patent owner retains title. The two primary types are exclusive licenses (which transfer all use rights to the licensee while the licensor keeps ownership — effectively granting a monopoly to the licensee) and non-exclusive licenses (which allow the licensee to produce and sell the invention, but the licensor and other parties can do so simultaneously). The video outlines the benefits: licensing generates royalty income, limits the inventor's risk by delegating manufacturing and marketing responsibilities, and can introduce the invention to much larger markets through established distribution networks.

The standard licensing process involves identifying potential licensees, having them sign confidentiality agreements, negotiating terms, and executing a formal license agreement that specifies scope, duration, geographic territory, royalty rates and payment structure, audit rights, and termination conditions. Common pitfalls include failing to require performance milestones (which allows a licensee to sit on the rights without commercializing), setting royalty rates too low, and choosing the wrong licensee without proper due diligence.

## Key Takeaways

- A patent license allows others to use a patented invention under defined terms while the patent owner retains title.
- Exclusive licenses grant one licensee all use rights; non-exclusive licenses allow multiple parties to use the invention simultaneously.
- Licensing generates royalty income without requiring the inventor to manufacture or market the product.
- The licensing process: identify candidates, sign NDAs, negotiate terms, execute a formal agreement.
- License agreements must specify scope, territory, duration, royalty rate and payment terms, audit rights, and termination conditions.
- Performance obligations are essential — without them, a licensee can hold the license without actively commercializing the invention.
- Setting royalty rates too low is a common and costly mistake.
- Have a patent attorney review any license agreement before signing.
