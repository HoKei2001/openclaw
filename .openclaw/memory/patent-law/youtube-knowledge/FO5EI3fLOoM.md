## Overview

This USPTO-hosted webinar brings together a panel of intellectual property experts — including attorneys, entrepreneurs, and IP educators — to discuss the when, why, and how of licensing intellectual property. The session covers all major forms of IP including patents, copyrights, trademarks, and trade secrets, providing a broad and practical overview of licensing as a business strategy for creators and inventors who want to monetize their work without manufacturing or selling products themselves.

The panel discusses the distinction between exclusive and non-exclusive licenses, emphasizing that a license allows the patent holder to grant usage rights while retaining ownership. A key theme throughout is that having protectable IP is not sufficient — that IP must have genuine commercial value for licensing to be a viable revenue strategy. Panelists stress the importance of performance obligations in license agreements (requirements for the licensee to meet milestones or the license can be terminated), appropriate royalty rate setting, and the critical role of carefully vetting potential licensees before entering into agreements.

The webinar also looks ahead to emerging challenges in IP licensing, including the impact of AI-generated content, digital distribution, and the need for broader IP education to help more entrepreneurs and creators understand and leverage their intellectual property rights effectively.

## Key Takeaways

- IP licensing allows rights holders to grant usage rights while retaining ownership, generating revenue through royalties.
- An exclusive license transfers all use rights to one licensee; a non-exclusive license allows multiple licensees simultaneously.
- Licensing transfers manufacturing and marketing responsibilities to the licensee, reducing the inventor's risk.
- Performance obligations in license agreements are critical to prevent a licensee from sitting on the rights without actively commercializing.
- Royalty rates must be set appropriately — rates that are too low significantly limit the inventor's return.
- Carefully vet potential licensees before signing; have a patent attorney review any license agreement.
- Having registered IP is a necessary foundation for licensing, but the IP must also have real commercial market value.
- IP licensing applies to patents, copyrights, trademarks, and trade secrets — not only patents.
