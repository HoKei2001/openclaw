# Copyright vs Patent vs Trademark

**Channel**: Elizabeth Potts Weinstein
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ruJkc1HTq3g
**Published**: 2020-11-02

---

## Overview

Attorney Elizabeth Potts Weinstein delivers a clear, accessible explanation of the three primary forms of intellectual property protection in the United States — copyright, patent, and trademark — helping small business owners and creators understand which framework applies to their situation and what each one actually protects.

Copyright protects creative works that have been fixed in a tangible format: books, articles, music (including lyrics, composition, and MP3 files), visual art, photographs, and logos or illustrations. Under US law, copyright protection attaches automatically when the work is created and fixed in tangible form — no registration is required. However, registering with the US Copyright Office at copyright.gov provides additional remedies and enforcement advantages. Copyright protection lasts for the life of the creator plus 70 years for most types of works.

Patents grant the inventor a government-issued exclusive right to make, use, and sell an invention for a fixed period — 20 years from filing for most utility patents. Utility patents cover functional inventions (processes, machines, manufactured articles, and compositions of matter), design patents cover ornamental appearance, and plant patents cover new plant varieties. To be patentable, an invention must be novel, non-obvious, and useful, and the application must fully disclose the invention in sufficient detail for someone skilled in the relevant field to replicate it. This disclosure requirement means patents cannot protect ideas that are not yet practically realizable. For some innovations used internally, trade secret law may be a better alternative than patent protection. Trademarks protect words, phrases, symbols, and logos used in commerce to identify the source of goods and services — brands like the Nike swoosh and the slogan "Just Do It" are examples. Trademarks can be maintained indefinitely as long as the mark is used in commerce and renewal fees are paid.

## Key Takeaways

- Copyright protects creative works and attaches automatically upon creation; registration adds legal enforcement advantages.
- Copyright protection lasts for the creator's lifetime plus 70 years; patents last 20 years from filing; trademarks can be maintained indefinitely with continued use.
- Patents cover functional inventions (utility), ornamental designs (design), and new plant varieties (plant), but require full public disclosure of the invention.
- An invention must be novel, non-obvious, and useful to qualify for a utility patent, and must be described in enough detail for others to replicate it.
- Patents cannot protect ideas that cannot yet be practically implemented — the invention must be reducible to practice.
- Trade secret law is an alternative to patent protection for internal business processes, offering theoretically indefinite protection without public disclosure.
- Trademarks protect brand identifiers used in commerce and can last indefinitely through continued use and periodic maintenance filings.

## Best For

Small business owners, entrepreneurs, and creators who need a clear starting-point explanation of the differences between copyright, patent, and trademark to determine which type of protection applies to their intellectual property.
