## Overview

Stephen Key of inventRightTV interviews Brian Schneier, a patent attorney who previously worked as a USPTO patent examiner, to get an insider perspective on how the patent examination process really works. The conversation reveals that patent examination is not purely a merit-based legal process — examiner incentives, personality, and the point-based performance system all significantly influence outcomes, often in ways that patent applicants never see.

Brian explains the USPTO examiner count system: examiners earn points for completing cases, with allowances scoring higher than rejections on first action. However, fear of quality control errors drives many examiners to issue initial rejections even when the invention is likely patentable, because approving cases too quickly without thorough searching can result in reprimands. This creates a predictable pattern where roughly 85% of applications receive an initial non-final rejection — a "false negative" — that applicants must respond to.

The conversation covers several actionable strategies: looking up individual examiner statistics (allowance rates, disposals per allowance, interview-to-allowance rates) to tailor your prosecution approach, using examiner interviews strategically based on the examiner's known receptiveness, considering appeal when the examiner has a very low allowance rate, and using Track One (fast-track) examination to get priority treatment. Brian also discusses how filing multiple focused applications can be more effective than cramming everything into one application with a restriction requirement.

## Key Takeaways

- Patent examination is influenced by examiner incentives and personality, not just legal merit — examiners are human beings operating under a point system.
- Examiners earn more points for allowances than rejections but often issue initial rejections out of caution to avoid quality control reprimands.
- Approximately 85% of applications receive an initial non-final rejection — this is normal and expected, not a sign that the invention is unpatentable.
- Look up individual examiner statistics (allowance rates, interview receptiveness) to inform your prosecution strategy.
- Some examiners respond better to interviews; others are easier to persuade through written arguments — tailor your approach accordingly.
- Track One (expedited examination) applications receive faster and more thorough examination and have notably higher allowance rates.
- Filing multiple focused applications instead of one large application with restriction requirements can result in broader overall coverage.
- Working with a patent attorney who understands examiner dynamics and uses data to guide strategy provides a significant advantage.

Inventors and patent applicants who want to understand the internal dynamics of USPTO examination and how to use that knowledge to improve their chances of obtaining a strong patent efficiently.
