# Patentability - Novelty & Obviousness

**Channel**: WittenLawLtd
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=faWsnaj8cno
**Published**: 2011-08-16

---

## Overview

This engaging video from WittenLawLtd uses horse boarding — a niche sport combining elements of mountain boarding, chariot racing, skateboarding, and water skiing — to illustrate the concepts of novelty and obviousness in patent law. Filmed near Wolverhampton, England, the video features two attorneys (one U.S., one UK) interviewing actual horse boarders to explore whether the sport's equipment and techniques could be patented.

The core distinction explained: an invention is novel if the exact thing hasn't been done before, and non-obvious if the differences between the invention and prior art would not be apparent to a person skilled in the relevant art. Horse boarding's harness attachment system provides the key example — while the individual components (mountain boards, water ski handles, quick-release mechanisms) all exist separately, the specific harness for connecting a rope to a horse's saddle (because no one had previously wanted to be towed by a horse in this way) appears to be genuinely novel. The inventors were advised they had one year from the video's disclosure to file a patent before losing rights.

The video discusses the U.S. vs. EU/UK approach to obviousness: U.S. law (non-obviousness) and European law (inventive step) are not identical. The European approach requires the examiner to identify what technical problem is solved by adding the distinguishing elements, then assess whether solving that problem by those means would be obvious. The presenters note that when combining more than three technical elements from different disciplines, a judge or jury is unlikely to find the combination obvious — a practical litigation insight. They also mention a real case involving adding password protection to a SATA storage device: the concept was useful but not novel, because it simply applied an existing technology in a predictable way.

## Key Takeaways

- Novelty asks: has the exact combination been done before anywhere? If not, the invention may be novel
- Obviousness asks: would a person skilled in the relevant art expect this combination to work or think to do it?
- The "person skilled in the art" varies by field — a horse rider and a skateboarder would assess horse boarding's obviousness differently
- Combining three or more elements from different technical disciplines tends to make the combination harder to find obvious in litigation
- European inventive step requires identifying the objective technical problem solved by the distinguishing feature, then assessing if the solution would be obvious
- U.S. Supreme Court confirmed (at time of recording): clear and convincing evidence is required to invalidate a patent on obviousness grounds
- Disclosure of an invention to the public triggers a one-year U.S. window to file a patent before all rights are lost

## Best For

Inventors and patent newcomers who want a memorable, real-world illustration of novelty and obviousness concepts using an unconventional sport as a relatable case study, with comparative U.S. and European perspectives.
