## Overview

Patent attorney J.D. Houvener delivers a short, focused explanation of the three fundamental legal requirements that every invention must satisfy in order to be granted a patent in the United States. These three criteria — novelty, non-obviousness, and utility — are the gatekeepers of the patent system, and failing any one of them will result in a patent application being rejected.

Novelty means the invention must be entirely new to the world. It cannot have been publicly disclosed before — not through prior patents, publications, products on the market, or any other form of public knowledge. Non-obviousness means the invention cannot simply be an obvious or trivial variation of something that already exists. Even if an invention is new, if a person with ordinary skill in the relevant technical field would consider it an obvious modification or combination of existing ideas, it will fail this requirement. The term "prior art" refers to all the existing technical references — both patent documents and non-patent literature — that an examiner can use to evaluate novelty and non-obviousness. Utility requires that the invention have some demonstrable benefit — functional, economic, mechanical, or even entertainment value — to justify granting monopoly rights.

Houvener presents these three requirements in a concise format that allows inventors to quickly self-assess whether their invention is worth pursuing before investing in a patent application.

## Key Takeaways

- Three requirements must all be satisfied for a patent to be granted: novelty, non-obviousness, and utility.
- Novelty: the invention must be entirely new — not previously patented, published, or publicly disclosed anywhere in the world.
- Non-obviousness: the invention cannot be an obvious variation or combination of existing prior art.
- Utility: the invention must have a demonstrable benefit — functional, economic, mechanical, or other practical value.
- "Prior art" includes all existing patents, published applications, scientific papers, and any other public disclosures relevant to the technology.
- Patent examiners actively search for prior art to challenge novelty and non-obviousness claims.
- All three requirements must be satisfied simultaneously; failing any one results in rejection.
