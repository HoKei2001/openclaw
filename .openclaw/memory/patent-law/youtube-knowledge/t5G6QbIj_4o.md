## Overview

This video from Patent Insanity explains the two primary legal grounds patent examiners use to reject patent claims at the USPTO: 35 USC Section 102 (novelty) and 35 USC Section 103 (obviousness). The presenter notes that receiving a rejection on virtually all claims in a first office action is expected and nearly inevitable — this should not discourage applicants. Understanding both grounds is essential for crafting effective responses.

Under Section 102 (novelty), the examiner must find a single prior art reference — a patent, published patent application, technical paper, or any other prior disclosure predating the filing date — that explicitly describes every element (called "limitations") of the claimed invention. The examiner must point to the specific passages in that reference that describe each limitation. If the examiner cannot show all elements in a single reference, the 102 rejection fails. A successful 102 response typically involves arguing that the cited reference does not actually teach one or more of the claimed limitations, or amending the claim to add a narrowing limitation that the reference does not disclose.

Under Section 103 (obviousness), the examiner is permitted to combine multiple prior art references. In a typical obviousness rejection, the examiner cites one reference teaching limitations A and B, and another reference teaching limitation C, then argues that a person of ordinary skill in the art would obviously combine them to arrive at the invention. The examiner typically provides a "motivation to combine" argument. Obviousness rejections are harder to overcome — the presenter estimates about 90% of prosecution arguments involve Section 103 rejections. Responses can argue against the combination itself (wrong field, teaches away, different purpose) or add further limitations to the claim.

## Key Takeaways

- First office actions almost always reject all claims — this is normal and expected, not a fatal blow
- Section 102 (novelty): requires a single prior art reference teaching every element of the claim; missing even one element defeats the rejection
- Prior art can be any public disclosure: patents, applications, technical papers, blog posts, YouTube videos, product catalogs — any technology disclosure
- Section 103 (obviousness): allows the examiner to combine multiple references; the examiner must argue why combining them would be obvious
- Approximately 90% of prosecution arguments are against obviousness (103) rejections, not novelty (102) rejections
- To defeat a 103 rejection: argue the combination is wrong, references teach away from each other, or add a new limiting element to the claim
- Responding to 102 rejections is generally easier than 103 rejections because you only need to challenge one reference

## Best For

Inventors and patent applicants who have just received their first office action and need to understand the two main types of rejections — novelty and obviousness — and what they can do about them.
