# What Makes an Invention Patentable?

**Channel**: John Ferrell, Esq.
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b6Mnko6YTF4
**Published**: 2023-07-27

---

## Overview

This video from patent attorney John Ferrell provides a clear explanation of the legal requirements an invention must satisfy to be patentable under U.S. patent law. The video is framed as a practical guide for inventors who want to assess their invention's chances before investing in the patent process, walking through each patentability requirement in accessible, non-technical language.

The video covers the three core patentability requirements: novelty, utility, and non-obviousness. Novelty requires that the invention not have been disclosed, sold, or publicly used anywhere before the effective filing date — with the important caveat that U.S. law provides a one-year grace period for the inventor's own disclosures, but no grace period for third-party disclosures that predate the filing. Utility is described as a relatively low bar: the invention simply needs a real-world use, and USPTO rejections on utility grounds are rare except in specific categories like perpetual motion machines (which the USPTO treats with heightened skepticism as a category that violates known laws of physics).

Non-obviousness is discussed as the most nuanced and frequently contested requirement. The standard asks whether a person of ordinary skill in the relevant field (POSITA) would consider the invention an obvious combination or modification of existing prior art at the time of the invention. The video explains that examiners frequently reject applications as obvious by citing multiple prior art references in combination, and that successfully overcoming such rejections requires demonstrating either that the combination was not obvious to try, that it achieved unexpected results, or that it solved a long-felt but unresolved need in the field. The video also briefly mentions the one-year statutory bar: inventors who publicly disclose, sell, or offer to sell their invention must file within one year or lose patent rights permanently.

## Key Takeaways

- The three patentability requirements are novelty, utility, and non-obviousness.
- Novelty requires no prior disclosure, sale, or public use before the filing date — inventors have a one-year grace period only for their own prior disclosures.
- Utility is a low bar: the invention must simply have a real-world use; perpetual motion machines are a hostile category.
- Non-obviousness is the most frequently contested requirement, evaluated from the perspective of a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA).
- Examiners often reject applications as obvious by combining multiple prior art references; overcoming such rejections requires strong argumentation.
- Unexpected results, long-felt need, and failure of others are secondary considerations that can support non-obviousness.
- The one-year statutory bar: any public disclosure, sale, or offer to sell starts a one-year clock to file before rights are permanently lost.
- Conducting a prior art search before filing helps inventors assess novelty and non-obviousness in advance.

## Best For

Inventors evaluating whether their invention meets the legal requirements for patentability before committing time and money to the patent application process.
