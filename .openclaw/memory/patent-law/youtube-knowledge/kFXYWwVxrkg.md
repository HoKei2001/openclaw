## Overview

This Patents Demystified video explains the two pathways to filing a utility patent — starting directly with a non-provisional application vs. starting with a provisional — and makes a strong case for startups to begin with a provisional. The presenter walks through the full timeline for each route, covering the USPTO examination process, the back-and-forth negotiation with examiners, and why the provisional's flexibility and cost deferral align better with startup development cycles.

## Key Takeaways

- A non-provisional patent application is the formal filing with required sections (claims, detailed description, formal drawings) and should generally be attorney-drafted.
- After filing, applications wait one to three years before examination begins, depending on the art unit's backlog (AI/computer technology is especially slow).
- The examination process involves an office action with rejections (expected ~95% of the time), followed by responses from the applicant — a negotiation to achieve the broadest allowable patent.
- The goal is the broadest patent that is "just barely" novel and non-obvious over the prior art; narrow patents are easy to design around and have little value.
- A provisional patent application is a placeholder — less formal, no claims required — that lasts one year and must be followed by a non-provisional to stay alive.
- Provisionals allow startups to defer costs, continue R&D, and add new developments to the non-provisional, providing incremental protection at a lower upfront investment.
- A staggered provisional strategy (filing a second provisional at 6 months) can capture multiple stages of R&D before converting to a non-provisional.
