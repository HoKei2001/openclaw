Patents protect inventions and provide
inventors with [music] exclusive rights
for a limited time. But not everything
can be patented, and qualifying for
patent protection requires meeting
specific criteria established by law. To
obtain a patent, an invention must
satisfy four fundamental [music]
requirements. It must have a useful
purpose, qualify as novel, be nonobvious
to someone skilled in the relevant
field, and fall within categories of
patentable subject matter. When
applying, inventors need to describe
their invention thoroughly enough for a
patent examiner to understand it. Though
a physical prototype isn't necessary,
[music] patentable subject matter
includes several categories. New plant
varieties created through asexual
reproduction can receive [music]
protection. Compositions or formulas
such as chemical compounds or mixtures
are eligible. Machines with moving parts
or circuitry qualify, as do processes
that accomplish tasks more efficiently.
Even simple tools designed for specific
purposes can receive patent protection.
Many specific items fall within these
categories. [music]
Business methods, computer software and
hardware, games, internet technologies,
jewelry, machines, magic tricks, makeup,
musical instruments, perfumes, plants,
and sporting goods can all potentially
be patented if they meet the other
requirements. Emerging fields have
expanded what can be patented. [music]
Biotechnology inventions like
genetically modified organisms may
qualify if they result from human
[music] intervention. Medical devices
and diagnostic tools often receive
protection, though surgical methods
themselves [music]
cannot be patented. Artificial
intelligence applications solving
technical problems may be eligible while
algorithms alone are not. Software
inventions must produce tangible results
and business methods need technological
implementation to qualify. [music]
However, certain things remain outside
patent protection. Abstract ideas cannot
be patented regardless of how
revolutionary they might be. [music]
Mathematical formulas, laws of nature,
and naturally occurring substances are
ineligible. Scientific principles,
physical activity processes like dance
routines, surgical methods, dangerous
drugs, criminal inventions, [music]
and concepts violating scientific laws
cannot receive patents. The government
also excludes inventions based on public
policy considerations. The patent office
rejects applications for inventions
contrary to public morality, those
posing significant safety risks, and
innovations violating laws or treaties
regarding human embryos or certain
genetic modifications.
Utility patents, often called patents
[music] for invention, protect
functional aspects and last 20 years
from filing. They cover production
processes, manufactured items,
compositions, machines, and improvements
[music] to existing inventions. To
qualify, the invention must demonstrate
usefulness, originality, and
non-obviousness.
Design patents protect ornamental or
aesthetic elements rather than
functional features. They last 15 years,
but offer narrower protection than
utility patents. When possible,
inventors should consider applying for
both utility and design patents to
protect all aspects of their creation.
Plant patents specifically protect new
plant varieties. Botonists must prove
their plant is novel, non-obvious, and
produced through asexual reproduction.
These patents also last 20 years. The
novelty requirement means showing that
no one has previously created the
invention. Applicants must verify no
earlier patent exists, the invention
isn't already public, no published
applications exist, no public versions
are available, and the inventor hasn't
previously sold the invention. These
standards collectively form what patent
law calls prior art. The United States
uses a first inventor to file system,
meaning patent rights go to the first
person to file an application, not
necessarily the first to invent. This
makes timing crucial. Inventors should
avoid [music] public disclosure before
filing and consider provisional
applications to secure early filing
dates while refining their inventions.
For utility patents, the invention must
demonstrate usefulness by providing some
benefit or helping [music] complete
specific tasks. This standard is
relatively easy to meet as even novelty
items can qualify as useful. Only
utility patents must satisfy this
requirement. Design and plant patents do
not. Patent applications must include
detailed written descriptions, [music]
enabling others skilled in the field to
make and use the invention. This
enablement requirement ensures the
inventor had possession [music] of the
complete invention when filing. The
nonobviousness standard requires that
the invention wouldn't be apparent to
someone with ordinary skill in the
relevant field. It must represent an
inventive step and produce unexpected or
surprising results. This subjective
standard can be challenging to meet as
patent examiners must determine what
would have been obvious at the time of
invention, [music]
not when they review the application.
Secondary factors can help establish
non-obviousness, including commercial
success, addressing long-standing needs
where others have failed, [music] or
producing unexpected results. Patents
provide valuable protection for
inventors, but navigating the
application process requires
understanding these complex [music]
requirements. Knowing what can be
patented and how to qualify is essential
for anyone seeking to protect their
intellectual property.
