What is the difference between patent
novelty and non-obviousness?
Have you ever wondered what makes an
invention truly unique in the eyes of
the law? Today, we are going to break
down the important concepts of patent
novelty and non-obviousness.
These two terms are essential for anyone
looking to secure a patent for their
invention, but they mean different
things.
First, let's talk about novelty. Patent
novelty means that your invention must
be new. This means that the exact
invention has not been known, used, or
disclosed anywhere in the world before
you file your patent application. If
there is even one prior art reference
like an earlier patent or publication
that fully describes your invention, it
lacks novelty. In simple terms, if your
invention is already part of public
knowledge, it cannot be patented.
Now, what about non-obviousness?
This is where things get a bit more
complex. Even if your invention is new,
it must also show an inventive step that
would not have been obvious to someone
with ordinary skills in the relevant
field at the time you created it. This
means that your invention cannot just be
a simple or predictable combination of
existing ideas. Non-obviousness prevents
patents from being granted for trivial
changes that experts could easily figure
out.
Your invention should representing a
meaningful advancement or an unexpected
feature compared to what was already
known. To clarify the difference,
novelty asks if your exact invention has
been disclosed before. If it has, you
cannot get a patent. On the other hand,
non-obviousness asks if your invention
would have been an obvious step for
someone skilled in the field based on
existing knowledge. If it would be
obvious, then no patent is granted.
When patent examiners review
applications, they first check for
novelty by comparing your invention to
prior art for exact matches. If your
invention passes this test, they then
look at non-obviousness.
They consider whether your invention as
a whole would have been obvious given
the prior art and common knowledge in
the field. Understanding these
differences is vital when you are
drafting patent applications or
responding to any rejections from the
patent office. To show novelty, you need
to demonstrate that no single prior art
reference discloses your invention
completely. For non-obviousness, you
should explain how your invention solves
problems or achieves results in ways
that were not suggested by prior art.
In summary, novelty protects the newness
of your invention while non-obviousness
safeguards its inventive quality. Both
are fundamental to securing a valid
patent. So, if you are working on an
invention, make sure it meets both
criteria to increase your chances of
getting that patent.
