Alright where to start? The US Patent and
Trademark Office is an agency of
the U.S Department of Commerce,
the USPTO for short, and they're the ones who
grant patents in the United States. Their main
web page [www.uspto.gov] has a lot of useful
information on it including a multi-step search
strategy and this will be our guide today.
Alright so this is our roadmap. First is to
brainstorm terms describing your invention.
This is a great initial step when looking
for scholarly articles as well. Then we'll do a
keyword search in Patent Public Search and review
those documents we find. The remaining steps are
about expanding your search and you can do this
with CPC classifications, reviewing the cited
references, and by looking for foreign patents,
non-patent literature, and or getting
help from a patent professional.
So, looping back now to step one – brainstorming.
This is the same first step as we take when
looking for articles. Think about your invention
or product, including the purpose, composition,
and use, and some of the synonyms, or component
parts of it. The example I’ll be using is a
unicycle frame. Other terms that might be helpful
include, bicycle (those are pretty familiar),
frame, wheels, pedals, and a seat are component
parts to a unicycle, and those might be helpful
too. Most of the time we aren't really sure
what will return helpful results at this stage,
and that's okay we'll likely find other
terms once we start poking around.
Step two is using Patent Public Search - that's
the name of their search interface - to do a keyword
search. This is a screenshot of what the web page
looks like and the address is at the bottom of the
screen [www.uspto.gov/PatentPublicSearch]. This
interface allows you to search with keywords or
with CPC codes, which I’ll mention again later.
It's through here that you can access the full
text of the patents, and I have a separate
video on demonstrating an example search.
Step 3 - conduct an in-depth review of
the documents found in your search. This
is essentially reading over what you have found.
I have a video on reading U.S. patents that goes
into more detail on this. Very briefly, the main
parts of the patent to look over are the drawings,
the specifications - which includes a description
of the invention, and the claims - which that the
limit for what the patent does and doesn't cover.
If you'd like to hear more about reading patterns
I have another video where we'll discuss this.
Step four - expand the search with relevant CPC
classifications. The CPC stands for Cooperative
Patent Classification. I have a separate video on
finding these, but briefly they group like things
together in a hierarchical structure. You can use
these as keywords for searches and help identify
similar inventions that might be helpful for you.
Step five. Similar to when you are researching
scholarly articles, looking at what the author
or inventor used as a basis for their work is
a great way to find similar research. These
are very easy to find on U.S. patents - they're
right on the front page and when you have those
patent numbers searching for them is very simple.
Step six - broaden your search even further. The
European patent office is one of the largest
in the world and it's a great place to look
for international patents. Espacenet is the name
of their search interface. This is of course not
the only other patent office in the world, there
are others to explore. You can also search for
non-patent literature in library databases,
and there are some that actually allow you
to search by patent number to find articles that
discuss that patent. For patent applications, there
are also patent searching professionals that
can provide additional guidance or services.
Super quick review now to wrap up. We have

stages. The basic searching and reviewing
and then if necessary expanding the search
