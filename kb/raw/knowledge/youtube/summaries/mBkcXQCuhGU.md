# Common Provisional Patent Application Mistakes

**Channel**: inventRightTV
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mBkcXQCuhGU
**Published**: 2018-08-24

---

## Overview

This video from inventRightTV features a conversation between host Andrew Krauss and inventor-friendly patent attorney Damon Kelly, who has been practicing patent law longer than provisional patent applications have existed. The discussion focuses on the most common and damaging mistakes inventors make when filing their own provisional patent application.

The single biggest mistake, according to Kelly, is paralysis — being so intimidated by the process that the inventor never actually writes anything. His prescription is to treat the application like telling a story to a family member: describe your invention the way you would excitedly explain it to your spouse or parent, and you will be 90% of the way there with only minor cleanup needed. The second mistake is using trade names or trademarks instead of generic descriptive language — calling a drain cleaner the "De-Clogger" rather than a "drain cleaning implement" introduces trademark language that has no inherent technical meaning in a patent context. Similarly, using the invention's marketing name throughout the application should be avoided.

The video also addresses the mistake of using inconsistent terminology — calling the same component a fishing pole in one sentence and a fishing stick in another creates ambiguity. Kelly stresses that inventors must fully disclose the secret sauce of the invention; the provisional application is never publicly examined, so there is no risk in being complete. Finally, language that is too specific — saying "made of polyethylene" when it could equally be made of metal or other polymers — unnecessarily narrows the protection. Inventors should use broad functional language while noting specific examples, and should not describe their invention in terms of color unless color is functionally significant.

## Key Takeaways

- The most common mistake is paralysis — avoid it by treating the application as a story told to a non-technical family member.
- Do not use trade names, product names, or marketing language in a provisional application; use generic descriptive terminology instead.
- Use the same terminology consistently throughout — calling the same component different names creates legal ambiguity.
- The invention's full technical details (the "secret sauce") must be disclosed; the provisional application is never publicly examined, so there is no risk.
- Avoid overly specific material or component descriptions — say "made of polyethylene or other polymeric, metallic, or natural materials without limitation" rather than just "made of polyethylene."
- Do not describe the invention in terms of color unless color has a specific functional role.
- A good provisional application is typically three to seven pages with three to five drawings that label and number each component.

## Best For

First-time inventors who are drafting their own provisional patent application and want to avoid the most common language, terminology, and disclosure mistakes that reduce the application's legal value.
