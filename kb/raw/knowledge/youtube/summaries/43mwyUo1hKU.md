## Overview

This concise explainer from Trademark and Patent Law Experts clarifies the distinction between two of the most important — and commonly confused — patentability requirements: novelty and non-obviousness. While both relate to comparing an invention against prior art, they ask fundamentally different questions and are assessed differently during patent examination.

Novelty (the simpler of the two) asks whether the exact invention has ever been publicly disclosed before the filing date. If any single prior art reference — a prior patent, published article, product description, or any other public disclosure anywhere in the world — completely describes the invention, then it lacks novelty and cannot be patented. The test is binary: either a single reference teaches every element of the claimed invention, or the novelty requirement is satisfied.

Non-obviousness is more nuanced: even if an invention is novel (no single reference teaches all its elements), it still cannot be patented if it would have been obvious to someone with ordinary skill in the relevant field based on all the existing knowledge available at the time. This means non-obviousness is judged holistically against the prior art as a whole, not against any single reference. The invention must represent a meaningful advancement or unexpected feature that an ordinarily skilled practitioner would not have easily predicted or arrived at. Patent examiners evaluate both criteria in sequence: novelty first, then non-obviousness. Understanding both is critical when drafting applications or responding to rejections.

## Key Takeaways

- Novelty asks: has this exact invention been publicly disclosed before? One prior art reference teaching all elements destroys novelty
- Non-obviousness asks: would this invention have been obvious to someone with ordinary skill in the field? Combines multiple references
- Non-obviousness requires a meaningful advancement — not just a trivial or predictable combination of existing ideas
- Patent examiners check novelty first (single reference, exact match), then non-obviousness (holistic, combinable references)
- An invention can be novel (no single prior art reference describes it) but still be obvious (obvious to combine two or more references)
- To show novelty: demonstrate that no single prior art reference fully discloses your invention
- To show non-obviousness: explain how your invention achieves unexpected results or solves problems not suggested by prior art
