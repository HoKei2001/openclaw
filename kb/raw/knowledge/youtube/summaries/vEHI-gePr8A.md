# How to Patent An Invention Idea

**Channel**: Patents Demystified
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vEHI-gePr8A
**Published**: 2021-02-23

---

## Overview

This Patents Demystified video addresses a common question that trips up inventors before they even begin: can an idea be patented? The presenter clarifies the distinction between what types of subjects can be protected by patents versus other forms of intellectual property, then directly challenges the widespread misconception that ideas cannot be patented.

The video first establishes what patents protect: utilitarian inventions — specifically methods (novel sequences of steps, including software processes) and things or systems (physical configurations, computer systems, compositions of matter). By contrast, artistic works such as books, films, and photographs are protected by copyright, while business names and logos are protected by trademark. Once an inventor confirms their idea falls within patentable subject matter, the relevant question shifts to whether the idea meets the requirements of novelty and non-obviousness.

The presenter pushes back against the advice that "ideas are not patentable." While technically true that a bare abstract idea cannot be patented, this framing is unhelpful because what most inventors have — an idea for a specific product or method — is very much patentable, even if it is still at an early stage. The actual threshold is low: the inventor must be able to describe the idea in sufficient detail that a person of ordinary skill in the relevant field could make and use it. The presenter cautions against rushing to file too early: the United States now uses a first-to-file system, which creates competitive pressure, but inventors should still develop their idea to a reasonable level and wait until they are approaching public disclosure, public use, or offers for sale before filing the first application. This timing prevents unnecessary early expenditure and protects against forfeiting patent rights through accidental public disclosure.

## Key Takeaways

- Patents protect utilitarian inventions — methods of doing things and physical or digital systems — not artistic or brand-related works (those are covered by copyright and trademark, respectively).
- The claim that "ideas cannot be patented" is misleading; most concrete ideas for products or processes are patentable as long as they can be described with enough detail for someone skilled in the field to implement them.
- The US now uses a first-to-file system, meaning the first person to file a patent application has priority — not the first person to invent.
- Despite the first-to-file system, inventors should not rush to file immediately; develop the idea and wait until approaching public disclosure, public use, or an offer for sale before filing.
- Key patentability requirements are novelty (no single prior reference discloses the invention exactly) and non-obviousness (the invention would not have been obvious to a person of ordinary skill in the art).
- A patent application must describe the invention in enough detail that a person of ordinary skill in the relevant field can make and use it — a surprisingly achievable standard for most concrete inventions.
- Consulting a patent attorney before filing helps determine whether a prior art search makes sense and whether the timing of the application is appropriate for the stage of development.

## Best For

Inventors who have a product idea and are uncertain whether their concept is patentable or are unclear about what the patent process actually requires before the first filing.
