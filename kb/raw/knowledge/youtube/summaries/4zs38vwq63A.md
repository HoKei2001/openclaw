# How to Patent Software

**Channel**: John Ferrell, Esq.
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4zs38vwq63A
**Published**: 2022-10-27

---

## Overview

Silicon Valley patent attorney John Ferrell explains the challenges and strategies involved in obtaining software patents in the post-Alice era. The video opens with a historical account of how the 2014 Supreme Court decision in Alice Corp v. CLS Bank dramatically changed the software patent landscape. Before Alice, the late 1990s and early 2000s saw a surge of broad software patents — often covering trivial applications of computers to well-known business processes — which gave rise to "patent troll" companies that bought these weak patents and used them to extract settlements from legitimate businesses. Alice introduced a two-part test for patent eligibility that attempted to eliminate abstract-idea patents, which initially made it very difficult to get any software patent allowed at all.

Ferrell shares that while the USPTO's application of the Alice test has relaxed somewhat over the years, important lessons remain. The first key strategy is to think of a general-purpose computer as being "specialized" by the software steps that program it — linking software operations to specific hardware elements such as memory structures, databases, or processing units makes the invention feel less abstract. The second strategy is to emphasize what the computer can uniquely accomplish that could not be done any other way (e.g., detecting malware on a network). The third strategy, highlighted as highly effective, is to identify feedback loops in the software — where prior results are used to update calculations in a continuous improvement cycle — as these form the basis of learning systems and AI, which Ferrell finds are highly patentable elements.

## Key Takeaways

- Software patents became significantly harder to obtain after the 2014 Alice Corp v. CLS Bank Supreme Court decision.
- Alice rejections (Section 101) occur when the USPTO determines the invention is merely an abstract idea implemented on a generic computer.
- Avoid referring to the invention as a "software patent" in the application; frame it as a machine or system claim instead.
- Tie software operations to specific hardware elements (memory, databases, processors) to make claims less abstract.
- Emphasize what only a computer can do — tasks that would be impossible for a human to perform manually.
- Identify feedback loops in the software where previous results improve future calculations; these are highly patentable elements.
- The USPTO's interpretation of Alice has relaxed since 2014, but strategic drafting is still essential for successful prosecution.

## Best For

This video is most useful for software engineers and technology entrepreneurs who want to understand the legal history of software patents and learn specific strategies for drafting patent applications that can survive Alice-based rejections.
