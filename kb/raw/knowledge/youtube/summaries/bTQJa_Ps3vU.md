## Overview

This introductory video from Bold Patents Law Firm walks inventors through the complete conceptual framework for patenting an idea, covering the key legal and strategic questions an inventor must address before and during the patent application process. The presenter, J.D. Houvener, begins with the most fundamental distinction: an idea alone cannot be patented — what can be patented is a specific, concrete implementation of an idea that qualifies as an invention under U.S. patent law.

The video covers five key areas: patent eligibility (whether the invention falls within the statutory categories of patentable subject matter), inventorship (correctly identifying all people who contributed to the conception of the invention), ownership (the difference between inventor rights and employer/assignment rights), statutory bars (the one-year grace period and the dangers of public disclosure before filing), and patentability (the novelty, utility, and non-obviousness requirements). The inventorship discussion is particularly important — listing the wrong inventors on a patent application can invalidate the patent, and employers should be aware that inventors who assign their rights under employment agreements do not lose their inventorship status.

The video also addresses international protection through the PCT process, explaining that inventors who have global commercial aspirations should plan their filing strategy early to preserve international rights within the required 12-month window. The presenter emphasizes that inventors do not need to have a fully working prototype to file a provisional patent application, but they do need to be able to describe the invention in sufficient detail to satisfy the written description and enablement requirements. The video concludes by recommending that inventors consult with a patent attorney early in the process to assess the full landscape before investing in a filing.

## Key Takeaways

- An idea alone cannot be patented — it must be a specific, concrete invention within the statutory patentable categories.
- The four statutory categories are: processes, machines, manufactures, and compositions of matter.
- Inventorship must be correctly identified — listing wrong inventors can invalidate the patent.
- Ownership (who holds the patent rights) is separate from inventorship and is often governed by employment or assignment agreements.
- Statutory bars include the one-year grace period rule: public disclosure, sale, or offer to sell starts a one-year filing clock.
- Patentability requires meeting novelty, utility, and non-obviousness requirements.
- A working prototype is not required to file, but the invention must be described with enough detail to meet written description and enablement standards.
- PCT filing within 12 months of the priority date preserves international patent rights for inventors with global market interest.
