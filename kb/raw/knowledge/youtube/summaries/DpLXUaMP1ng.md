# Software Patents, And Why We Have Them

**Channel**: OnSoftware
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DpLXUaMP1ng
**Published**: 2011-07-28

---

## Overview

This video provides a foundational explanation of why software patents exist and why they benefit society. It traces the history of the patent system in the United States back to the Constitution, explaining that before patents existed, inventors kept their innovations secret. When those inventors died, their knowledge was lost, hindering technological progress. The patent system was created as a social contract: inventors receive a government-granted monopoly for a set number of years (17–20) in exchange for publicly disclosing exactly how their invention works. After the monopoly expires, anyone can use the invention freely.

The presenter argues that this same rationale applies to software. Just as a steam engine can be patented both as a physical device and as a method (the process of heating steam to drive pistons), software — which is fundamentally a method in its purest form — can and should be patented. The video pushes back against the tendency of software developers to underestimate the uniqueness of their work, noting that a lot of software is genuinely novel and that patents are one of the strongest available tools for protecting it.

The key insight offered is that software represents a method of doing something, and methods have long been patentable in U.S. law. The video encourages software creators to consider patent protection seriously rather than relying solely on secrecy or copyright, because patents force public disclosure while providing legal protection — benefiting both the creator and the broader technological ecosystem.

## Key Takeaways

- The U.S. patent system was created as an exchange: public disclosure of an invention in return for a time-limited monopoly
- Before patents, inventors kept innovations secret; when they died, knowledge was often lost with them
- Software is essentially a method in its purest form, and methods have always been patentable in U.S. law
- Software developers often underestimate the novelty and uniqueness of their work — many software inventions qualify for patent protection
- Patents protect not just physical devices but the underlying processes and methods, which is exactly what software represents
- After 17–20 years, patented inventions enter the public domain and anyone can use or build upon them
- Patenting software encourages public disclosure rather than secrecy, which advances technological progress

## Best For

Software developers and engineers who are new to the concept of software patents and want to understand the philosophical and legal basis for why software can and should be patented.
