## Overview

This McGill University continuing education webinar features IP attorney and patent agent Natalie Raful — co-chair of McGill's IP courses, managing partner of Ottawa-based Brianne Rafal, and a recognized expert in patenting and licensing software with an electrical engineering background. The session covers software patentability with particular focus on artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies in both U.S. and Canadian patent law contexts.

The webinar begins with patent fundamentals: patents are territorial rights granted for novel, non-obvious, and useful inventions that enable the public to understand the technology in exchange for a time-limited monopoly. Raful explains the specific challenges of patenting software and AI/ML systems, noting that the post-Alice landscape in the U.S. makes abstract software claims difficult — applications must demonstrate that the invention provides a specific technical improvement to a computer or technical field rather than merely automating an abstract idea or business process. Canadian patent law has similar challenges following the Supreme Court of Canada's Amazon decision.

The session covers strategies for drafting AI/ML patent applications that survive examination, including focusing claims on specific technical implementations (e.g., a novel neural network architecture, a specific training method, a particular way of processing sensor data), demonstrating improvement to computer function or hardware performance, and linking algorithmic innovations to concrete technical effects. Raful discusses the importance of disclosing training data characteristics, model architecture details, and implementation specifics in the specification to create durable patent protection that competitors cannot easily design around. A Q&A session covers practical questions from attendees about protecting ML models, trade secrets as an alternative, and filing timing strategy.

## Key Takeaways

- AI/ML inventions are patentable if they provide a specific technical improvement — not just an abstract algorithm or business logic automation
- Post-Alice (U.S.) and post-Amazon (Canada) decisions make it critical to frame software claims around technical implementations, not abstract processes
- Focus claims on specific neural network architectures, novel training methods, or concrete hardware-software interactions with measurable technical effects
- Include detailed technical disclosures in the specification: training data characteristics, model architecture, and implementation specifics
- Trade secrets are an alternative to patents for AI/ML where the algorithm itself is the core value — but trade secrets are lost if the product is reverse-engineered
- Filing before public disclosure is essential — even in jurisdictions with grace periods, early filing is best practice
- Strong patent applications for AI/ML technologies must demonstrate that the invention improves computer functionality, not just performs a mathematical task

## Best For

AI/ML researchers, software developers, and technology company IP counsel who want a nuanced, practitioner-level understanding of how to patent artificial intelligence and machine learning innovations in the U.S. and Canadian patent systems.
