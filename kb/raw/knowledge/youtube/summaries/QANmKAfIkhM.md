## Overview

inventRightTV host Stephen Key explores a fundamental conflict of interest between inventors and patent attorneys: inventors want to make money from their ideas, while patent attorneys are focused on protecting those ideas — two different goals that do not always align. Stephen argues that inventors should ask better questions when evaluating potential patent attorneys, going beyond simply asking how many patents they have obtained.

The key question Stephen recommends asking is: "Can you show me some of the products you have protected that have actually succeeded commercially?" He explains that truly great patent attorneys will have clients who come back repeatedly because their work helped those clients achieve real business success. If an attorney cannot produce success stories (beyond citing NDA restrictions as an excuse), that is a red flag worth heeding.

Stephen emphasizes that the best patent practitioners build long-term relationships with inventors by being invested in their commercial success, not just charging fees for filings. He also promotes inventRight's "Gateway" program for inventors who need help finding and pitching the right companies for licensing deals, offering a full-service alternative for those who want guidance beyond just the patent filing process.

## Key Takeaways

- There is an inherent conflict of interest between inventors (who want commercialization) and patent attorneys (whose goal is protection).
- Ask patent practitioners not just how many patents they have issued, but how many of their protected inventions have actually succeeded in the market.
- Truly effective patent attorneys will have success stories and returning clients — proof that their advice helped inventors make money.
- Beware of practitioners who cannot or will not share any examples of commercially successful products they have helped protect.
- Good patent attorneys are long-term partners, not one-time service providers — their business grows when yours does.
- Inventors should evaluate patent attorneys based on commercial outcomes, not just filing credentials.
