## Overview

This video by UpCounsel provides a structured overview of patent licensing as a strategy for inventors to monetize their inventions without manufacturing or distributing products themselves. It covers how to identify potential licensees, what a licensing agreement must include, the different types of licenses available, how to prepare a patent for licensing, and how to compare licensing versus selling a patent outright.

## Key Takeaways

- Patent licensing allows inventors to earn royalties while a third party handles manufacturing and distribution; the inventor retains ownership of the patent
- Four key steps to licensing a patent: identify potential manufacturers with strong distribution channels (via trade shows, databases, or stores), develop compelling marketing materials, present to targeted manufacturers, and negotiate a favorable licensing agreement
- Publishing a notice in the USPTO Official Gazette can announce a patent's availability for licensing, but most successful deals come from direct, targeted outreach
- Licensing agreements must be drafted by an intellectual property attorney and should address: scope of license, term and termination conditions, royalty structure (upfront fees, milestone payments, ongoing royalties), performance milestones, infringement handling, improvement rights, and confidentiality
- There are six major types of patent licenses: exclusive (sole rights to one party), non-exclusive (multiple parties), cross-licensing (two parties exchange licenses), sublicensing (licensee can grant sub-licenses), field-of-use (restricted to specific markets), and territorial (restricted to specific geographic regions)
- Inventors do not need to wait for a granted patent to begin licensing; patent pending products can be licensed successfully when commercial viability can be demonstrated, with a clause specifying rights become active upon patent grant
- Common licensing mistakes to avoid: insufficient research into potential licensees, weak or vague agreements, sharing technical details without protection, overvaluing the patent, and trying to handle complex legal matters without professional help
