# Common Patent Application Mistakes - Humans In the Claims

## Overview

This Inventor's Quick Tips episode addresses a specific and common patent drafting mistake: including human actors as elements in patent claims. Using a weather-retrieval app as the example, the presenter demonstrates how claims drafted with language like "receiving a key press from a user" or "sending the weather data to the user" introduce a human into the claim, which can create several legal problems. Human activity is generally not considered patentable on its own, and human-centric language often causes claims to be oversimplified, skipping intermediate hardware steps or otherwise failing to fully describe how the system works technically.

The solution is to reframe human actions in terms of the technical system components involved. A "key press from a user" becomes a "key press from a display (touch screen) of a mobile electronic computing device." "Sending data to the user" becomes "sending data to an electronic computing device." The revised claims describe the same functionality without the human, making them more technically complete, more clearly patentable, and less vulnerable to rejection on grounds of claiming non-patentable human activity.

This principle applies broadly to any invention where humans interact with technology: always ask whether the human's role can be captured by describing the hardware or software component that mediates the interaction, and write the claim to claim that component rather than the human.

## Key Takeaways

- Including human actors in patent claims is a common drafting mistake that should be avoided where possible
- Human activity is often not considered patentable, so claims with humans in them are weaker
- Human-centric claim language tends to oversimplify, skipping technical steps or hardware that should be claimed
- Reframe human actions in terms of the hardware or software components involved (e.g., "touch screen of a mobile electronic computing device" instead of "user")
- The claim should describe how the invention works technically, not just what the user does at the interface level
- This principle applies to any invention where humans interact with technology
- Removing humans from claims typically results in more technically complete and legally sound claims
- Always ask: can this human action be replaced with the technical component that performs or receives it?
