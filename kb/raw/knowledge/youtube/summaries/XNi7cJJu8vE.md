## Overview

This video from Bold Patents Law Firm provides a clear explanation of the two-part framework inventors must understand before filing: patent eligibility and patentability. While these terms are often confused, they address distinct legal questions. Eligibility asks whether the type of invention can be patented at all, while patentability asks whether this specific invention is new and non-obvious enough to deserve patent protection.

On the eligibility side, the video explains the categories that are excluded from patent protection: abstract ideas (including mathematical formulas and mental processes), laws of nature (such as gravity or thermodynamics), and inventions that are already patented or in the public domain. These exclusions exist because granting monopolies on fundamental principles would stifle innovation rather than promote it. The video also clarifies the four statutory categories of eligible subject matter: processes, machines, manufactures, and compositions of matter.

For patentability, the video walks through the three core requirements under U.S. patent law — novelty (the invention must be new), utility (it must be useful), and non-obviousness (it cannot be an obvious extension of what already exists). The non-obviousness standard is framed from the perspective of a "person of ordinary skill in the art" (POSITA), meaning the invention must represent a meaningful leap beyond what that hypothetical skilled person could readily combine from existing knowledge. The video encourages inventors to conduct a preliminary prior art search before investing in a full patent application.

## Key Takeaways

- Patent eligibility and patentability are two separate legal questions that must both be satisfied.
- Ineligible subject matter includes abstract ideas, laws of nature, and natural phenomena — regardless of how novel they are.
- The four eligible statutory categories are: processes, machines, manufactures, and compositions of matter.
- Novelty requires that no prior art discloses every element of the claimed invention.
- Utility is a low bar — the invention must simply have a real-world use.
- Non-obviousness is evaluated from the perspective of a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA).
- Conducting a prior art search before filing helps assess patentability and avoid wasted application costs.
- Inventors should consult a patent attorney to assess both eligibility and patentability before committing to the filing process.

## Best For

Inventors and entrepreneurs at the early stage of the patent process who need to understand whether their idea qualifies for patent protection before spending money on an application.
