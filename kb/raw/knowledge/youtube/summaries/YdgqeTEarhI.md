## Overview

Dylan Adams, a patent attorney at a large firm, opens this video with an unusual disclaimer: he explicitly states he is not offering this advice to attract clients and will not accept inquiries from viewers — a framing designed to signal that the advice is genuinely unbiased. The video covers both the standard and less-obvious criteria for choosing a patent attorney, with particular emphasis on the insight that cheap options often end up costing more in the long run.

The most important starting criteria are verification and focus: only registered patent attorneys or agents can represent inventors before the USPTO, and that registration can be confirmed on the USPTO website. Beyond registration, Adams argues that the attorney should spend at least 90% of their time drafting patent applications, planning portfolios, and working through examination — not splitting time across trademark, litigation, or transactional work. Technical background must match the invention: the two main practice areas are biotech/chemistry and computers/electrical, with physical/mechanical products falling into a more flexible middle ground that most experienced attorneys can handle. He cautions against expecting a perfect technology match and focuses instead on the broader domain alignment.

The business-type fit is another critical dimension: attorneys who primarily serve Fortune 100 companies are not set up to work effectively with startups or individual inventors. Billing structures may be misaligned, and such attorneys may lack the communication style needed to guide founders without in-house legal support. Two insider tips stand out: (1) find someone excited to work with you but not desperate — attorneys who need clients urgently may not act in the inventor's best interest; and (2) treat the attorney as a long-term trusted team member rather than a document-processing vendor, since the relationship spans provisionals, non-provisionals, examination, and ongoing portfolio management over multiple years. Finally, Adams emphasizes that low billable rates or flat-fee arrangements often result in worse outcomes through lower quality work, dragged-out examination processes, and death-by-a-thousand-cuts billing.

## Key Takeaways

- Verify registration on the USPTO website before engaging any attorney; unregistered attorneys cannot represent inventors before the USPTO
- An attorney should dedicate at least 90% of their practice to patent prosecution (drafting, portfolio planning, examination) — not divided across litigation, trademarks, or transactions
- Technical domain alignment matters: biotech/chemistry and computers/electrical are the two main clusters; mechanical products are more flexible but still require relevant experience
- Big-firm attorneys serving Fortune 100 clients are typically ill-suited — and often uninterested — in working with startups and individual inventors
- Find an attorney excited to work with you but not desperate; desperation can lead to advice that prioritizes billing over your best interests
- Treat the relationship as a multi-year strategic partnership, not a one-time transaction — the same attorney handles provisionals, conversions, examination, and ongoing portfolio strategy
- Low billable rates and flat fees often produce worse outcomes than higher rates from experienced attorneys who work more efficiently and provide better strategic advice

## Best For

Inventors and startup founders who want candid, insider-perspective guidance on how to truly evaluate and vet a patent attorney beyond surface-level factors like price and firm size.
