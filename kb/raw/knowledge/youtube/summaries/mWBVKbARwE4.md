## Overview

Patent attorney and author Dylan Adams of Patents Demystified provides a thorough explanation of obviousness rejections under 35 USC 103, how they arise during patent examination, and the two primary strategies for overcoming them: amending claims and arguing against the rejection. The video gives practical guidance on how inventors can actively contribute to their own patent prosecution and avoid the trap of getting a patent that is technically issued but effectively worthless due to overly narrow claims.

## Key Takeaways

- Obviousness rejections under 35 USC 103 arise when an examiner combines two or more pieces of prior art whose elements together cover all the elements of the claimed invention; the legal standard is whether a person of ordinary skill in the relevant field would have found the claimed combination obvious
- Unlike novelty rejections (35 USC 102), which require a single reference to teach all claim elements, obviousness rejections can use many references combined — sometimes five or six — making them much harder to overcome
- Applicants have two main options for responding: amend the claims to differentiate from the prior art by adding language that narrows or clarifies the invention, or argue that the examiner's combination is wrong (e.g., the prior art doesn't actually teach a claimed element, the references are not analogous art, or the examiner misread the references)
- Claim amendments can only add language already supported in the original specification and drawings; a poorly written non-provisional application limits the available fallback positions — this is one of the most important reasons to hire a patent attorney for the initial filing
- Patent examination is a negotiation: the goal is to get the broadest possible claims, not just any claims. A patent with very narrow, highly specific claims may technically issue but provide no real protection because no competitor could ever infringe it
- Inventors can actively assist their patent attorney by going through the prior art references cited by the examiner element by element, identifying anything that the cited prior art does not actually teach, and communicating those differences clearly
- After a successful response, the examiner may conduct a new prior art search and raise new rejections based on the amended or clarified claims; the back-and-forth typically continues until the examiner either allows the claims or the applicant appeals
