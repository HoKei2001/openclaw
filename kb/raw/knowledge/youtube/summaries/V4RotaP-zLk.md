# Understanding Patent Eligibility and Criteria

## Overview

Patent attorney J.D. Houvener of Bold Patents Law Firm provides a concise explanation of the two-stage framework for evaluating whether an invention can receive patent protection: eligibility and patentability. These are distinct hurdles that every invention must clear before a patent can be granted.

The eligibility stage asks whether the invention fits within the categories of patentable subject matter under patent law. Most inventions qualify, but there are exceptions: abstract ideas (concepts that are too intangible to grab onto or enforce), laws of nature (natural phenomena, newly discovered equations or physical principles that cannot be personally owned), and inventions that have already been patented and entered the public domain. Everything else — including machines, processes, articles of manufacture, and compositions of matter — generally passes this first test.

The patentability stage requires satisfying three criteria for utility patents: novelty (the invention must be the first of its kind in the world), non-obviousness (it cannot be an obvious variation of something that already exists in the prior art), and utility (it must have a present benefit or use). Houvener briefly outlines how inventors should approach evaluating these criteria, starting with their own research and then consulting a patent attorney for a legal opinion on whether the invention is truly new and non-obvious.

## Key Takeaways

- Patent protection requires clearing two distinct stages: eligibility (can it be patented at all?) and patentability (does it meet the three criteria?).
- Excluded from eligibility: abstract ideas, laws of nature, natural phenomena, and already-patented inventions in the public domain.
- The three patentability criteria for utility patents: novelty (new in the world), non-obviousness (not an obvious variant of prior art), and utility (has a benefit).
- Most inventions pass the eligibility test — the eligibility exclusions are narrower than many inventors assume.
- DNA sequences, natural phenomena, and mathematical equations are examples of things that cannot be patented even if newly discovered.
- Inventors should do their own preliminary research on novelty before consulting a patent attorney to confirm patentability.
- Non-obviousness requires showing the invention is not just a minor, predictable tweak to something already known.

## Best For

Inventors who are at the very beginning of the patent process and want a clear introduction to the eligibility and patentability requirements before deciding whether to pursue a patent.
