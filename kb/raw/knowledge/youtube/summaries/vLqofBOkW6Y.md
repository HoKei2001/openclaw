## Overview

Patent attorney Dylan Adams (Patents Demystified) provides a thorough explanation of the obviousness standard of patentability and how it is applied during USPTO examination. The video establishes the context: examination only applies to non-provisional patent applications, which wait 1–3 years in the USPTO queue before a patent examiner reviews them. When examination begins, the examiner performs a prior art search and evaluates whether the claimed invention is both novel and non-obvious.

The core test for obviousness is: would it be obvious to a person of ordinary skill in the relevant technology field to arrive at the claimed invention based on all known prior art? Unlike novelty, which requires a single prior art reference to teach all elements of a claim, obviousness allows the examiner to combine multiple references. For example, if the invention has elements A, B, C, and D, the examiner might cite one reference containing A and B, and another containing C and D, and argue that it would be obvious to combine them. This is the most common type of rejection in patent prosecution.

To rebut obviousness rejections, inventors can argue directly against the combination (e.g., the references teach away from each other, or the combination would not work) or point to secondary considerations: commercial success of the invention, long-felt need in the industry that went unsolved, failure of others to solve the problem, or unexpected results that those skilled in the art would not have predicted. Concrete evidence — publications, sales data, expert testimony — is more persuasive than anecdotal arguments. The video also notes that the law changed in 2007 (KSR v. Teleflex), making obviousness rejections easier for examiners to issue by eliminating the requirement that prior art explicitly suggest combining references.

## Key Takeaways

- Obviousness is judged from the perspective of a person of ordinary skill in the relevant technology field at the time of the invention
- Unlike novelty rejections (single reference, all elements), obviousness rejections can combine multiple prior art references
- About 95% of patent applications receive at least one rejection — the majority are obviousness rejections
- Secondary considerations can rebut obviousness: commercial success, long-felt need, failure of others, and unexpected results
- The 2007 KSR v. Teleflex decision eliminated the requirement for explicit motivation to combine references, making obviousness rejections easier
- Concrete evidence (sales data, publications) is more persuasive than anecdotal arguments when rebutting obviousness
- Amending claims to narrow or clarify limitations is an alternative strategy when arguments alone are insufficient
