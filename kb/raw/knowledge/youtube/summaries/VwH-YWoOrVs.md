## Overview

This technical deep-dive from Patent Insanity explains how patent examiners construct novelty (Section 102) and obviousness (Section 103) rejections, and how patent attorneys analyze and respond to them. The video builds on the companion piece covering the two main grounds of rejection and focuses on the practical mechanics: how prior art references are cited, what the examiner points to in the specification, and how arguments or amendments can be constructed to overcome rejections.

For novelty rejections, the examiner must find specific language in the specification (not the claims) of a cited prior art reference that describes each limitation (A, B, and C) of the claimed invention. The examiner cannot simply assert the reference teaches a limitation — they must point to specific passages. If the cited language does not actually support the mapping, a patent attorney can argue against the examiner's reading. If the reference genuinely anticipates the claim, the best response is usually to amend the claim by adding or clarifying a limitation that the reference does not disclose.

For obviousness rejections, the examiner combines multiple references (e.g., Reference 1 teaches A and B, Reference 2 teaches C) and argues that a person of ordinary skill would combine them. The examiner typically provides a motivation to combine — explaining why someone in the field would think to merge the teachings. About 90% or more of prosecution arguments involve obviousness rejections, which offer more complex but also more varied avenues for response: arguing the combination itself is illogical, that the references teach away from each other, that there was no motivation to combine, or (like novelty) amending the claim to distinguish from the combination.

## Key Takeaways

- Examiners look at the specification and description of prior art references, not their claims, when mapping limitations
- Novelty (102) rejection: examiner must map all limitations (A, B, C) to specific language in one reference — missing any one defeats the rejection
- Obviousness (103) rejection: examiner combines multiple references and provides a "motivation to combine" argument
- Approximately 90%+ of prosecution arguments are against obviousness rejections — they are more complex to both construct and overcome
- For novelty rejections, either argue the examiner's reading of the reference is incorrect, or amend the claim with a distinguishing limitation
- For obviousness rejections: argue against the combination logic, lack of motivation, or that references teach away — or amend the claim
- Clarifying existing limitations (not just adding new ones) can sometimes be sufficient to overcome a novelty or obviousness rejection
