# Patent Claim Drafting - 5 Core Principles - 12 Common Mistakes

**Channel**: Rolf Claessen
**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uTgs0eNdzEc
**Published**: 2017-03-23

---

## Overview

Patent attorney Rolf Claessen presents a comprehensive tutorial on how to draft patent claims, covering general principles, a five-step drafting process, twelve common mistakes, and his personal preferred strategy. The video is structured as a practical guide for patent professionals and technically sophisticated inventors.

The general principles establish the mindset for good claim drafting: always have infringement in mind (if a feature cannot be proven to be present in an infringing product, that feature is useless in a claim), and always consider how an infringer could design around the claim and draft accordingly to make circumvention as difficult as possible. The five-step process moves from understanding the invention in detail, to searching for prior art and identifying the differences between the invention and the prior art, to drafting using the two-part form (a preamble describing the invention in its most generic form, followed by a body containing the one distinguishing feature or broadest distinguishing combination), to verifying that all claim features are physically or logically connected, to ensuring that all relevant claim types are covered — product claims, process claims, and use claims.

The twelve common mistakes include: multiple sentences in a single claim (a claim must be one sentence); misuse of the word "and" in a list of components; choosing transitional phrases incorrectly (preferring "comprising" over "consisting of" because "comprising" is an open list allowing additional elements while "consisting of" is closed); inconsistent terminology (always use the same word for the same concept); claiming the result rather than the means; too little or too much detail; dependent claims that broaden or contradict the independent claim; using trademark names in claims; overusing means-plus-function language; and incorrect use of articles (introduce a feature with "a" or "an," then refer to it subsequently as "the"). Claessen's personal strategy: conduct an excellent prior art search first, identify the broadest single distinguishing feature, and use that feature in the body of the independent claim.

## Key Takeaways

- Always draft claims with infringement in mind — if a feature cannot be detected in an infringing product, it has no enforcement value.
- Use the two-part claim form: a preamble with the most generic description, followed by a body with the single broadest distinguishing feature.
- Prefer "comprising" (open list) over "consisting of" (closed list) to maintain broader claim scope.
- Use the same terminology consistently throughout all claims — never use synonyms for the same technical concept.
- Each claim must be a single sentence; multiple sentences in one claim will trigger an objection for lack of clarity.
- Introduce new elements with "a" or "an" and refer to them subsequently with "the" (the antecedent basis rule).
- Do not claim the result; instead, claim the structural features or method steps that achieve the result.
