## Overview

This video from J.D. Houvener of Bold Patents Law Firm presents a detailed six-step framework for conducting a professional-grade patent search, explaining what a true patentability search entails and why a search report alone is not enough — what inventors really need is a patentability opinion from a licensed patent attorney.

The video begins by clarifying what patentability actually encompasses: eligibility (whether the invention falls within patentable subject matter under Section 101), novelty (whether the invention has been disclosed before), non-obviousness, and utility. Houvener emphasizes that cheap patent searches costing under $500 or $1,000 typically lack an attorney opinion and are therefore not useful for making a real filing decision. Step two focuses on invention disclosure — understanding the invention's history, any employer relationship that might affect ownership, and the full breadth of the invention including alternative embodiments. Step three narrows the invention down to three to five core keywords representing what the inventor believes is truly novel.

The fourth step uses these keywords to conduct a broad natural language search starting with Google, covering both patent and non-patent literature worldwide. Step five then narrows into patent-specific classification codes (CPC), searching within USPTO and international databases by technology class. Step six returns to non-patent literature — academic journals, Kickstarter campaigns, newspapers, and industry publications — to find any disclosure that could constitute prior art or suggest the invention is obvious. The video closes with a call to action encouraging inventors to download the free Bold Ideas Guide to Patents and consult with a Bold Patents attorney.

## Key Takeaways

- A true patentability search is not just a list of similar patents — it requires an attorney opinion evaluating eligibility, novelty, non-obviousness, and utility.
- Cheap patent search services (under $500–$1,000) typically lack the attorney opinion needed to make a real go/no-go decision on filing.
- The invention disclosure step surfaces important issues like employer ownership rights and the one-year public disclosure bar.
- Narrowing the invention to three to five core novel keywords is essential before searching databases efficiently.
- Start with Google's natural language search to cover the full universe of patent and non-patent literature before moving to classification-based searches.
- CPC classification codes allow targeted retrieval of all patents and applications in a specific technology area.
- Non-patent literature — journals, crowdfunding pages, blogs, and trade publications — can constitute prior art and must be included in a comprehensive search.
