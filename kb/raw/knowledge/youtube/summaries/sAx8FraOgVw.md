# Obviousness or Inventive Step - How the EPO Determines Inventive Step

**URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sAx8FraOgVw
**Published**: 2018-02-08

## Overview

European patent attorney Rolf Claessen explains how the European Patent Office (EPO) assesses inventive step (called "obviousness" in U.S. patent law) using the problem-solution approach. The video is specifically focused on how to construct an effective attack on a patent claim in opposition proceedings using the inventive step standard. Claessen clarifies that while the EPO uses "inventive step" and the USPTO uses "obviousness," the concepts are essentially equivalent.

The EPO's problem-solution approach has three steps. First, identify the closest prior art — typically a single document in the same technical field that is most similar to the claim being challenged, ideally differing in only one feature. Second, determine the objective technical problem — identify what advantage or technical benefit the differentiating feature provides. If the patent specification does not claim any particular advantage for the difference, or if none can be found, the objective technical problem defaults to "provide an alternative," which is the weakest possible framing and the weakest attack. Third, determine whether the skilled person in the art would have arrived at the claimed invention by modifying the closest prior art in light of the objective technical problem, typically by combining it with a second prior art document that teaches the differentiating feature (possibly from an adjacent technical field).

The car/rear-view mirror example used: a car with four wheels is the closest prior art; the differentiating feature is a rear-view mirror; the objective technical problem is avoiding collisions; a document about bicycles with rear-view mirrors for collision avoidance could motivate the skilled person to combine the references, making the invention obvious. Claessen emphasizes that the skilled person must not only be able to make the modification, but must have had a reason — an incentive — to do so.

## Key Takeaways

- The EPO uses "inventive step"; the USPTO uses "obviousness" — both assess whether the invention was non-obvious to a skilled person
- EPO's problem-solution approach: (1) find closest prior art, (2) determine objective technical problem, (3) assess if combination of references would be obvious
- Closest prior art should be in the same technical field and ideally differ by only one feature
- If no advantage is taught for the differentiating feature, the objective technical problem defaults to "provide an alternative" — the weakest attack
- A second document can be from a neighboring technical field if it teaches the solution to the technical problem
- The skilled person must not only be able to make the modification but must have had motivation (incentive) to do so
- This framework is used in EPO opposition proceedings to challenge the validity of granted patents

## Best For

Patent attorneys, patent agents, and technically trained practitioners who want to understand and apply the EPO's problem-solution approach for assessing or challenging inventive step in opposition or examination proceedings.
